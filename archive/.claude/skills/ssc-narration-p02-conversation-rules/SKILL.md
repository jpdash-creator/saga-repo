## Area 2 — Conversation Rules

### 2.1 — Mandate

Dialogue is not decoration. It is one of the two primary instruments of novel-grade literature — the other being interior prose. Every scene involving characters who are capable of speech and are in a position to speak must contain dialogue unless the narrative context renders silence itself the meaningful choice (which must then be rendered with the same depth as speech).

Minimal dialogue is a structural failure. Repetitive dialogue is a creative failure. Both are forbidden.

### 2.2 — Dialogue Formatting

**Rule 2.2.1 — Embedded in Paragraphs.**
Dialogue must be embedded within prose paragraphs. It is woven into the fabric of description, action, and interiority. Dialogue is never isolated as standalone lines without context. Before, within, or after each spoken exchange, the paragraph contains physical detail, psychological observation, environmental response, or some combination of these. The speech act is part of a complete experiential moment.

**Rule 2.2.2 — Written in Double Quotation Marks AND Italics.**
All dialogue is formatted in **double straight quotation marks AND italics simultaneously**. The canonical rendering is *"the exact spoken words"* — the double quotation marks and the spoken text are all rendered inside the italic span. This applies to direct speech of any length, from a single word to an extended speech of multiple paragraphs. Italics-only dialogue (no quotation marks) is a Rule 2.2.2 violation; double-quoted dialogue with no italics is a Rule 2.2.2 violation; single quotation marks for primary direct speech are prohibited (single quotes are reserved for quoted speech nested inside another character's spoken line, per Part 29 Rule 29.2.1.1a). Thought presented as interior monologue may also be italicized at the author's discretion, but interior monologue does NOT take double quotation marks; only spoken dialogue is double-quoted. This rule is jointly governed with Part 29 Rule 29.2.1.1 and both must be simultaneously satisfied.

**Rule 2.2.3 — No Script Format.**
Dialogue must never appear in script format — no character names followed by colons, no stage directions in brackets, no numbered exchanges. Dialogue attribution and context are handled entirely through prose convention: dialogue tags ("she said," "he demanded"), action beats (the character performing an action before or after speech), and contextual description (the environment responding to the speech act).

### 2.3 — Dialogue Content Requirements

**Rule 2.3.1 — Frequency.**
Conversations must be frequent within scenes involving multiple characters. The narrative must not spend extended passages in pure narration or action without incorporating the spoken voices of the present characters. When characters are together, they speak — unless silence is itself the point and is rendered accordingly.

**Rule 2.3.2 — Inclusion of All Characters.**
Dialogue must include all characters present in the scene who are relevant to the scene's events — heroes, antagonists, minor figures, mentors, rivals, bystanders who carry narrative weight. A scene in which only the protagonist speaks while others listen passively is a scene of failed characterization. Every present character has a voice, a perspective, and a reason to speak.

**Rule 2.3.3 — Emotional and Contextual Range.**
Dialogue must reflect the full spectrum of human communication. Within a given scene, dialogue may and should carry multiple of the following functions simultaneously or in rapid sequence:

- **Emotion:** Joy, grief, rage, love, contempt, tenderness, envy, despair, relief — genuine emotional states expressed through speech.
- **Conflict:** Disagreement, argument, struggle for dominance, ideological opposition, personal grievance.
- **Misunderstanding:** Characters hearing what they expect rather than what is said. Assumptions that reveal character. Crossed purposes that reveal the gulf between people.
- **Authority:** The language of rank, command, deference, challenge to power, the subtle assertion of superiority.
- **Fear:** Concealed dread, false confidence, the way fear reshapes what people choose to say and not to say.
- **Humor:** Wit, irony, dark comedy, gallows humor, teasing, the laughter that survives even terrible circumstances.
- **Doubt:** The hesitation before a hard truth, the question that reveals uncertainty, the statement that asks for reassurance without admitting it needs it.
- **Revelation:** Speech that changes what the listener believes. Confessions, accusations, truths delivered at the wrong moment.
- **Negotiation:** The bargaining of power, the careful dancing around what is really wanted.
- **Subtext:** What characters mean beneath what they say. What they will not say directly. The conversation beneath the conversation.

No scene's dialogue should be tonally flat or confined to a single register. Dialogue breathes because people breathe — with complexity, contradiction, and the full weight of who they are.

**Rule 2.3.4 — Prose Depth Match.**
Dialogue must match the depth of the surrounding prose. A scene written at the pacing-appropriate prose depth standard (3500%–5000%) cannot contain functional, expository dialogue that serves only to convey information. Every line of dialogue must carry the scene's emotional charge, reveal character, advance conflict, or layer subtext — ideally multiple of these at once.

**Rule 2.3.5 — Context-Driven Speech.**
All dialogue is governed by the full context of the scene: the characters' history with each other, their current emotional states, the political or social setting they are in, the stakes of the moment, and what each character knows, suspects, and is concealing. Speech without context is placeholder. Speech arising from full context is literature.

**Rule 2.3.6 — Dependency on Part 29.**
When dialogue is written, it must satisfy the requirements of **Canon-Part 29 — Dialog and Social Interaction Law** in addition to the rules of this Area. Canon-Part 29 governs tone registers, speech pattern variation by character type, cultural and social formality rules, and the mechanics of how conversation shapes and reflects the social world. This Area (Part 2, Area 2) governs formatting and frequency. Canon-Part 29 governs voice, content, and social authenticity. Both must be simultaneously satisfied.

**Rule 2.3.7 — Novel-Wide Punctuation and Register Prohibitions (Em-Dash and Contractions).**
All Prose body text written under this Part — both dialogue and narration — is subject to the following two categorical prohibitions authored in Part 29:

- **Em-Dash Prohibition (Part 29 Rule 29.2.1.1b):** The em-dash character (—) is categorically prohibited in the Prose body. The author must substitute a full stop and new sentence, a colon, a semicolon, a comma, parenthetical brackets, or a restructured clause. The en-dash is also not a valid substitute.
- **Contraction Prohibition (Part 29 Rule 29.2.1.1c):** Contractions (haven't, isn't, don't, I'm, you're, it's, that's, and all other apostrophe-elided forms) are categorically prohibited in the Prose body. All such forms must be rendered in their full expanded form (have not, is not, do not, I am, you are, it is, that is, etc.). Proper nouns, place names, and cultivation-world lexemes containing apostrophes for non-contraction reasons (possessives, transliterations) are outside the scope.

"Prose body" is defined as the text between the Prose Title Block and the end of the Prose file — the text the reader reads as the novel. Governance artefacts (Gate 1 Position Header Block, Chapter Header Block, Area Divider, Prose Title Block, Audit Reports, ledger tables, Cross-Part Dependency tables, and all Part-file structural content) are outside the scope of Rule 2.3.7. Audit Pass 1 (Rule 7.1.1(k) of Part 1) and Audit Pass 2 both FAIL if the em-dash character is found in the Prose body or if any prohibited contraction is found in the Prose body. This rule applies to every Prose produced under any of the 13 prose-producing commands.
