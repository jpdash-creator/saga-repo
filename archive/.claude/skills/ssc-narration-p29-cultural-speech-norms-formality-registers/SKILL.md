## Area 6 — Cultural Speech Norms and Formality Registers

### 6.1 — The Governing Principle

Every character's speech is shaped by the cultural framework in which they were formed — the Cultural Paradigm Type (CPT) designated in Part 25. Cultural speech norms govern: the appropriate formality of address, the expected rituals of conversation, what directness means, what politeness requires, what counts as rudeness, and how much weight is given to the unsaid versus the said.

**Rule 29.6.1.1 — Cultural Speech Norms Are Specific:** Part 25 defines the cultural context of every civilization and faction in the Starward Saga. Part 29 requires that those cultural contexts produce specific, differentiated speech patterns in characters from different cultural origins. Two characters from different cultural backgrounds speaking in the same generic style is a narration violation.

**Rule 29.6.1.2 — Cultural Collision in Dialogue Is Governed:** When characters from different cultural backgrounds interact, the collision of their speech norms is a narrative asset. What one character considers respectful, another considers cold. What one considers direct, another considers rude. What one expects as ritual preamble to any serious conversation, another considers unnecessary delay. These collisions reveal culture, reveal character, and reveal relationship — they are never to be smoothed over.

### 6.2 — Formality Registers

Dialogue in the Starward Saga operates across five formality registers:

**Register F1 — Ceremonial:** The highest formality register. Reserved for formal rituals, official declarations, the opening of important negotiations, the delivery of verdicts, the pronouncement of sect hierarchical decisions, and other moments when the occasion itself demands elevated language. Ceremonial dialogue often contains fixed phrases, traditional formulations, and ritual structures that mark its distance from ordinary speech. A character who fails to observe Ceremonial register when it is required commits a social failure with real consequences.

**Register F2 — Formal:** The register of professional and hierarchical address. Used when speaking to those above one's rank, in official contexts, and in encounters with strangers or rivals where social position is being established. Formal dialogue avoids contractions, uses titles and honorifics correctly, and maintains a controlled presentation of the self.

**Register F3 — Semi-Formal:** The everyday register of professional relationships — colleagues, seniors one knows well, junior members of one's own organization. Semi-formal dialogue allows more warmth, more individual expression, and more deviation from strict protocol while still observing the basic structure of hierarchical respect.

**Register F4 — Intimate:** The register of genuine personal relationship — close allies, trusted friends, family, romantic bonds. Intimate dialogue allows complete relaxation of formal structures. It is where the most honest, most vulnerable, and most self-revealing speech occurs. Characters in intimate register speak in ways that would be inappropriate in any higher register — and this vulnerability is itself a narrative statement about the depth of trust between them.

**Register F5 — Degraded:** The register of extreme emotional disruption — when the social architecture of speech has been overwhelmed by crisis, rage, grief, or psychological collapse. A character who drops into Degraded register from a higher register is communicating, through the very register collapse, that the situation has exceeded their capacity for social management. Degraded register is not disorder — it is a specific signal about what has happened to the character in this moment.

### 6.3 — Formality Violations as Narrative Events

**Rule 29.6.3.1 — Formality Violations Must Be Rendered:** When a character violates the expected formality register — whether by speaking above their station in an official context, by maintaining cold formality in a moment that calls for intimacy, or by dropping into intimate register with someone whose trust has not been earned — this violation is a social event. Other characters register it. The violation reveals something about the speaker's state, their values, or their intent. It may be intentional (a power move, a deliberate challenge) or unintentional (a failure of social management under pressure). Either way, it must be rendered.

**Rule 29.6.3.2 — Correctional Speech Is Governed:** When a formality violation occurs, other characters may correct it — or may choose not to correct it, which is itself a social act. The correction or non-correction, and the way it is delivered, is dialogue that carries all the registers simultaneously.
