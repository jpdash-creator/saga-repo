*Year 1246, Ulenor, Nuvil, Day 8*

The frost had come down early and settled hard, and by the last of the light the ruts rang under the iron on the wheels. Five wagons went down the lower road in file, nose to tail, and the breath of each team stood over it in a cloud the evening did not move. On the pole of the last wagon hung the banner, two cloths side by side from one crosspiece, the cultivators' sigil on the left cloth and the makers' sigil on the right, as every guild crew and household in the kingdom hung theirs.

Ansel Roke walked at the front of the file and kept his eyes on the road. It ran down the slope ahead of him, pale between a ditch on his left and the first trees of the Skyvein Forest on his right, and a little way on it narrowed to a width where two carts could not have passed. He knew that narrow as he knew his own stairs: sixty paces, with the file filling fifty of them. Below it stood the last toll-house, where the file would lie tonight in the keeper's yard, and under the covers of the first two wagons the two chests of shard, the hard crystal of packed qi the Bluecleft mine digs out, lay strapped down.

He counted the file again as he walked. Edric Gower on the first seat, Hobb Marlow on the second with Perrin Lisle, the clerk, beside him, Emmet Wicker on the fourth: five carters and the clerk, six who did not fight, and Roke's sense found nothing in them, only the blankness of bodies that had never taken qi in. Eight guards, and himself. Roke was the one Warrior in the mine's guard, ***Warrior Level 1 Mid***, and his soul sense, the inner reach by which a Warrior feels other men's qi, ran fifty paces, where his Seniors could feel another fighter's pressure only at arm's length, as a faint tightness under the ribs.

A boy from the toll-house came running up the slope beside the front wagon with a slip in his fist. Gower took the paper without slowing his team, and Roke read it over his shoulder. *"Narrow at dusk, yard by full dark,"* Gower read, and pushed it up his sleeve. *"It is the same as last month, near enough word for word. Do you think the frost lies on both sides of that narrow, or only on the shade side? The off wheel went out from under me there last time."*

*"The frost lies on both sides, by the look of the ruts,"* Roke said. *"So the lamps go up only when you reach the mouth of the narrow, not a pace before, and you walk the team in rather than drive it."*

*"Last month it was hurry or we miss the yard, and this month it is walk the team,"* Gower told his horses. *"I am beginning to think there is no pleasing a mine."* The boy was already running back down the road.

At the bend Roke looked back along the file and saw a man in a good cloth coat far up the slope behind the last wagon, walking away toward the ford with his hands in his sleeves, too far off for Roke's sense to reach. He gave him one look and went back to the road.

Below the ford the slope dropped into the narrow, and at the mouth of it Roke turned and set his guards with his hand and his voice. He kept the place in his mind as he would have drawn it on a slate, the road pale, the ditch and the trees dark, and nine dots to be placed. *"Davin, I want you on the ditch side, ahead of the first wheel, and Jessa will take the same place on the forest side, a spear's length in from the first trunks. Gareth comes with me at the front, and Wil can walk back along the trees to the third wagon and come up again. The rear pair stay with the banner and do not leave it for anything."*

Davin Tarn, ***Senior Level 8 Mid***, the best spear Roke had, went to the ditch side at once, his boot heel tapping the frost as it had tapped since the mine, a small steady knock Roke had long since stopped hearing. *"I will keep the ditch side with my spear low as always,"* he said, *"so that whatever comes out of those trees meets the point first."*

Across the road Jessa Lowe, a Senior of the sixth Level, shifted her grip. *"I will take the forest side again, then, and stand a spear's length off the trunks, as you ask,"* she said.

Wil Harker, ***Senior Level 9 Low***, had gone back along the trees, and his voice came up the file. *"Nothing moves in the trees on my side,"* it said, *"and nothing roosts in them either, which is a great deal quieter than I like."*

It was quiet, and that was the trouble with it. At this hour of a winter evening the trees along a road should have been loud with small birds going in to roost. Tonight they came over the ditch in their threes and fours, passed above the first trees, and did not go in. Roke counted fourteen. Pale lines showed in the ridges of the trunks as the light failed, and the hanging leaves of the *Skyveil* that closed the gaps between them stood white with frost and did not stir. He sent his sense into the trees to its full reach, and the frost and the hanging leaves dulled it as a thick glove dulls a hand, and it found nothing there but cold. His thumb lay on the mouth of the horn at his belt, the brass worn smooth by his own hand, and he could not have said when it had gone there.

As the file went into the narrow the carters lit the wagon lamps, and five small flames stood up yellow down the length of it. Gareth Sefton, ***Senior Level 7 High***, stamped his own feet at Roke's shoulder. *"If you cannot give that heel a rest, Davin, you will wake the whole forest,"* he said, *"and I would sooner it stayed asleep."* The knock went on.

The first thing that moved was the frost. It came off the hanging leaves along the first wagon in a single fall, the way it comes off a bough when something heavy goes through, and for a heartbeat nothing else moved at all. Davin's heel had stopped.

Then the screen of leaves parted and five men stepped out of the forest side by side, in one stride, the sacking falling from the tops of the long weapons in their hands. Brown and grey and undyed wool, the hafts bound in rag along the grip, and on none of them a badge, a cord, a colour or a word. Their pressure came over Roke's soul sense all at once, no longer kept down as it had been until that step, and the air went thick. Where the trees had been empty a moment before, five Warriors stood on the road, the nearest of the fourth Level or the fifth by the weight of him, every one far above Roke's own. Behind them the dark was not empty either; more stood in it, still kept in and quiet, a weight he could feel and not count.

A sixth Warrior burst from the trees ahead of the rest, low and fast along the forest side, the sacking falling off his halberd, a pole as long as he was tall with an axe edge on one side of its head, a hook behind the edge and a spike above. The weapon was already travelling: ***Rushing Axeblade***, stride and cut in one motion, so that the edge was across Jessa Lowe's forearm before her spear had come round. Her arm opened, and she stepped back with her good hand clamped over the cut and her face gone the colour of the road. *"He opened my arm from the wrist to the elbow, and I never saw his hands move... I never even saw them!"* she said through her teeth.

*"Get back to the wheel, Jessa, and squeeze that arm shut with your other hand!"* Roke called. *"Keep your feet under you whatever happens, and stay where I can see you!"*

The five went for the guards at the front of the file as if they shared one pair of legs, and there was only the breath of five men in the cold and the clack of wood on wood as the guards' spears came up. Not thieves; thieves shouted, and wore what they had stolen last.

On the front wagon Gower had half risen from his seat with the reins in his fists, talking to his team, low and fast. *"Whoa now, my beauties, steady,"* he was saying. *"It is only a fox in the road, and we will be in the yard before full dark."* A hook came up from the road beside the wheel and caught him under the arm, and the man behind it leaned along the haft: ***Levering Hookpull***. The iron bit into the folds of his coat and drew him off the seat, the reins following, and the road took him on his back beside the near wheel. Gower's hand moved on the ruts, opening and closing, and Roke saw it and had no time for it.

Before the fear came, before the sword, Roke counted what he had: eight guards and himself, and one of the eight out of it already. He tore the horn from his belt and blew, once, long and hoarse, and the note went out over the frozen ground. *"Carters, get under the wagons and lie flat, and keep your heads down whatever you hear!"* he shouted. *"The rear pair keep the banner whatever they see, and Gareth stays on my shoulder! Davin, hold that wheel until I reach you!"*

The nearest of the five was on Roke before he had finished turning. Roke cut as he turned, the saber coming round hard from the hip, and the haft met it, and the shock went up his arm and stayed there; the haft had not even shivered. The man pushed without hurry, and then drove his shoulder into Roke's breastbone on a short stride with his whole body behind it. ***Shoulder Hammer***. Roke's boots went back two paces on the frost, and Gareth's shoulder came up against his.

Gareth's spear went past Roke's ear at the man's belly, and the coat took it: ***Hide Brace***, the skin beneath it hardened and the feet set, so that the point skated off into the dark without giving a thread. Roke's arm had told him what his eyes had not: this one was stronger than he was, by a long way, and there was nothing to be done about that by being quick. *"He turned my spear aside as though it were a twig,"* Gareth cried, *"and I put my whole strength behind it!"*

*"Then do not meet him with the point again!"* Roke said between breaths. *"Go left, with the wheel at your back, and give ground slowly... every pace he takes has to cost him something!"*

Beside the lead team Davin Tarn held his place with his spear low. A halberd came over the lead horse's back, and the axe edge took him at the shoulder before he could turn from the wheel, and he went down on the ditch side with the spear still in his hand and his heel quiet. Roke said his name once, and the ditch gave him nothing back.

The horn's echo was thinning out across the frost, toward anyone near enough to hear it, when the rank leaned its whole strength on the front of the file.

Hadric Stoll's hand found the sacking on his haft while the horn's echo was still dying over the frozen ground. It went there as it had gone all evening, to rest on a thing it knew. Then he did his checks again, in the same order: the strip of paper in his belt, the knot that closed the top of the sacking, the lashing that bound the hook to the haft, the laces of his boots. All as they had been the last three times.

He saw only what moved. Stillness he took on trust, and so the six who crouched round him under the trees were six darker places in the dark, known by their small sounds, a creak of cloth, a breath let out, for the whole company kept its qi drawn in tight, and a Warrior's sense found nothing in a man who did that. Through the gap in the leaves the front of the file stood lit by its own lamps, where the five and the sixth man had the guards pinned against the lead wheel. Rask's hand had gone up and come down once, not long ago, and sent the first six through the leaves to hold the front; the second lift would send the rest. Stoll was a Warrior of the sixth Level, a squad leader, and where the guards fought at the wheel his soul sense picked them out as a thin quick prickle, Seniors all but one, the leader with the horn, whose weight stood just inside the Warrior realm.

The paper was the road. A strip as long as his hand, folded in three: the narrow at dusk, the yard by full dark, in a clerk's neat letters. A man in a good coat had put it into Stoll's palm in the last of the afternoon and gone off along the ditch without a word, and Stoll had watched his back the whole way, the one thing in all that cold that moved. The strip lay against his ribs, flat and dry.

The Redhaft Vanguard went to its work with nothing on it that named anything: coats bought at three markets, hafts bound in rag, no colour, no cord, no cap. Brackenhold's king had worn his crown three years, and a king three years crowned wants a first road. King Osgar Brack wanted this one, and the mine at the top of it, and the company had been told so when it was called together, in the king's own few words, and told one thing more: it must shed no soldier's blood, because a dead soldier would turn a raid into a war, and a war would name the company. The hour had come off the wire. Stoll knew that much and no more: a clamp on the wire at a relay hut by the ford, so that every notice the toll-houses sent each other about the file was read before it was delivered.

Beside him, near enough that the sacking on his sleeve scratched, Venn Orrick, ***Warrior Level 3 Low***, the youngest and the lowest of the squad, let out a cloud of breath, and the haft shook in his hands. *"They told us this was only the first road,"* he whispered, *"and that there would be a great many more after it, so I do not understand why I feel like this over the first."*

Brenn Stannard, ***Warrior Level 5 High***, did not turn his head. *"Then let your hands shake quietly until the sign,"* he said, *"because the road does not care whether it is the first or the fortieth."*

Stoll rebound his grip with the spare rag and spoke to the dark in front of him rather than to the six. *"I will say the order once more, so that nobody has to think when the sign comes. The wrap comes off your haft in the same pull that brings it forward, and the three at the front plant their feet across the road and cut as one. The rest of you come with me to the second wagon, the driver first and the lashings after, and the straps are cut at the buckle and never through the leather, because those chests leave here whole. The guards at the wheel are to be pinned and left, because every breath spent on a man who is not in the way is time the post can use. There will be no cries on the road and no names, and when the hand goes up and does not come down, you stop, whatever you have in your hands."*

Something moved near the third wagon, where the trees came down to the road behind the file. Until it moved there had been no Rask at all, only a place in the dark; he stood as the trees stood, his qi drawn in to nothing, and Stoll's sense had passed over that place twice and found only forest. He had counted the file in with his lips shut. Now his right hand rose again to the height of his shoulder and came down once, and his weight came up against Stoll's back as a hand might be laid flat between the shoulder blades. Rask stood at the very top of the Warrior realm, ***Warrior Level 9 Peak***, a hair short of Champion, and the whole company knew the number and nobody said it. Stoll's knees wanted to bend, and did not.

No word started them. Stoll pulled. The wrap came off the top of his halberd in one drag and fell behind him, and seven pulls made one long tearing sound of cloth, and the qi they had kept in came out with it: ***Brawnhaft***, and the first blow was already begun while the rag was still falling.

They went through the gap together, with the teams screaming ahead of them. The three at the front, line fighters of the fourth Level and the fifth, planted their feet across the road, and the qi ran down their arms into the edges, and they swung as one from the trees to the ditch in a single long flat sweep as wide as the road itself: ***Rubble Axefall***. The front of the file came apart before it. The guards in a short row at the lead wheel were struck back against it, and the driver who lay beside that wheel, in the path of the edge, did not move again. The guard who had blown the horn took the sweep across the ribs and went down on one knee with his sword hand flat on the frozen road, and his voice came thin and breaking above the rest. *"I have six still on their feet, and Jessa is at the wheel... Gareth, stay with her! Wil, wherever you are, come up along the trees to me!"*

*"The driver never raised a hand to anyone,"* Venn said, staring at the heap by the wheel. *"The edge went through him as though he were not there. I did not think it would be like this."*

*"You thought it would be like the drill, and it is not,"* Brenn answered steadily. *"So look at the work in front of you, and leave the rest for when we are back among the trees."* Venn's grip, Stoll saw, had stopped shaking.

The three who had swung stood with their sides heaving and their edges dulled, the price of the blow. *"Mine has gone blunt,"* said Alwin Lorne, on the left, panting, *"and it will not bite again for another ten breaths."*

*"Then keep behind us and use your haft,"* Dunstan Calder answered from the middle, *"and I will cover your side until the edge comes back."*

Down the tree side the guard called Wil came at a run toward the front, spear low, a Senior of the ninth Level running as well as a Senior can run. Rask was walking. It was the only walking on that road. He fell into the guard's stride for three paces as though the two had agreed to keep time and put the hook of his halberd behind the man's knee: ***Subjugating Hookback***. The hook closed, the knee went, and the guard's own speed threw him face down along the ruts. Rask did not look at what he had done. A fighter of the rear squad stepped over the fallen man and ended him with one plain blow, in silence.

Stoll took the second wagon. Its driver stood on the seat with a whip in his fist, and beside him the clerk sat rigid with a book pressed against his coat. *"Hobb, let them have the chests, I beg you!"* the clerk whispered. *"Get off my wagon, you thieves!"* the driver shouted. *"There is nothing on it for the likes of you, and I have a team to see to!"*

*"Come down off the seat now,"* Stoll said, almost gently, *"and I will see that nobody touches your horses."* His hands, which had been quick all evening, went careful. The hook found the seam under the driver's arm, a little lower than it needed to, and the haft came down through his palms in one long measured pull: ***Staticsnatch***, the man lifted and thrown over the hip off his seat onto the ruts, where he lay curled with a hand clamped to his shoulder. Stoll looked at the place where the hook had been. It was exact. He stood over it longer than the order allowed.

On the far side of the seat another hook found the clerk. *"Please,"* he said, to the air, to nobody. *"I am only the clerk, and the book is in order, and the seals are whole, so take them, take whatever you came for! I have a boy at home... I have never done anyone any harm."* A line fighter's ***Levering Hookpull*** drew him over the far rail, the book flying one way and the clerk the other, and the blows that followed were short and flat. When Stoll lifted his eyes from his own work the clerk lay across the ruts with both arms over his face, breathing.

The front was theirs. The guards who could still stand had backed against the lead wheel, and one of them kept saying, to himself or to the wheel, that somebody must have heard the horn and that they would come. Two of the line had begun on the cover lashings of the first wagon, and one put a knife through a strap instead of at the buckle. *"Cut at the buckle,"* Stoll said without turning, *"because those straps have to bear the chests when we carry them out, and a cut strap will not."* The knife moved. The chests lay under their covers, banded and sealed, four strides from his hand.

That was when the second horn spoke. It came from the road behind the file, from up toward the ford, and it was not the mine's horn: shorter and higher, two flat notes with no hoarseness in them, a soldier's horn. An answer. Not one man of the company turned; the line took it as it had taken everything, without a sound. But Stoll's order broke. It had a place in it for guards and none for soldiers. His hand went, without waiting for him, to the paper at his belt and lay flat on it, and only afterward did the rest of him turn toward the sound.

Rask's voice came down the trees, low and carrying, the first voice of the company to rise above a whisper since the horn. He had the road behind the file in sight. *"Eight are coming down the road behind us at a run,"* he said. *"Hold the front wagons where they stand, and take the third wagon before the runners reach it."* The rear squad rose out of the trees at the third wagon behind Leofwin Pellam, ***Warrior Level 4 Mid***, the dry-voiced man who led it, and ran.

One pair, two, three, four: eight backs ahead of Elowen Tregear, eight shield-boards slung across them, eight clouds of breath torn off and left behind. Hasler, ***Warrior Level 4 High***, ran at the front with the horn, and Ferris, ***Warrior Level 4 Mid***, led the third pair; the other six ran between the third Level and the fourth. Nine veterans picked for the shard road, and she was the strongest of them, ***Warrior Level 5 Peak***, the very top of the fifth Level. *"Keep your pair-mates at your shoulders and close up,"* she called, *"and let nobody run ahead of his pair."*

The road came out of the last bend at the ford, and below it lay the narrow with the whole file in it, five wagons nose to tail with their lamps lit. At the rear the banner still hung whole, two grey cloths stiff with frost. Far ahead men in plain coats held the front; nearer, on the third wagon and in the road beside it, stood more plain coats than a wagon carries. Their pressure reached the soul sense of all nine of them across the last fifty paces of the slope, and it told her what she had hoped it would not: Warriors, every one, those at the third wagon about as strong as her own pairs.

Between the third wagon's tail and the fourth wagon's nose lay a gap of four paces, where the fourth team had backed off. It was the only open place between the plain coats and the last two wagons; the ditch and the trees closed everything else. *"Hasler, Ferris, take the gap with your pairs across the mouth of it, shoulder to shoulder, and put the other two pairs close behind along the trees,"* she called. *"Take your boards off your backs now and put them on your left arms, and let nobody break the row for anything short of my word."*

*"Please... is anyone there?"* said a thin voice from under the fourth wagon. *"I would give anything for a drink of water."* She heard it and did not go. The gap came first.

She set her shoulder against Hasler's and her board against the nose of the fourth wagon, and let the brace go out from her down the row: ***Unmoving Backbrace***. It ran along the shoulders until the five boards stood as one and the pairs behind could lean on it.

The first cuts came down over the tops of the boards, and the soldiers answered them. They turned the blade as a sentry is taught at a gate and did not leave the place: ***Angled Counterguard***. Hasler and the man opposite traded blow for blow, and neither moved a foot.

*"That one went through the board and into my teeth, Captain, and I am very glad of the pair behind me!"* Hasler said between the second blow and the third.

*"My board is splitting along the grain, and I do not think it will stand another blow like the last!"* Ferris called.

*"Then let it split, and keep the sound half in front of your face,"* Tregear told him. *"Nobody gives a pace unless I say so."*

The first to reach the end of the row was the lowest of them; she knew it by how he came, a stride ahead and a little too fast. She stepped out of the row and met him with the cut that tells a stranger at a gate to stop, the saber's edge laid across his front a hand from his coat. *"Stand where you are!"* He stopped, a young man with his mouth working at the effort of saying nothing, and then he shoved her blade aside with his haft, and through the steel she read him, ***Warrior Level 3 Low***, well below her own. She took the opening the shove made, short and plain, and he went down on the frost with his thigh laid open. *"Stay down,"* she told him.

The second came from the ditch side right behind him, with the same pause and the same shove, and through the steel she read him, ***Warrior Level 3 Mid***, a little above the first. She took the same opening, and he fell on the lip of the ditch with his haft across him. The rest of the plain coats did not come at her. *"Leave the officer for last, because two of us have just paid to learn what she costs,"* a dry voice said among them. *"Break the boards first, since the boards are what keep the row together."* They came at the boards.

Behind them, at the tree side of the third wagon, a shape she had taken for a stump in the frost stirred. A man stood there with a halberd upright in both hands, and as the boards took their blows he raised it by degrees, and the frost lifted off the ruts around his boots, and the pressure behind him grew as the haft rose. Whatever he was gathering was meant for the whole road, and his own men stood between her and him. Ferris's board split down the grain and the next blow took his arm above the elbow; the fourth pair closed up behind him, and the row did not give.

Then the pressure stopped growing, and the butt of the halberd struck the ruts once, and the plain coats drew back, all at once and with no word, to the trees and the edge of the ditch, and the middle of the road lay open. The man walked into it and stopped. *"Nine stand across the road,"* he said, not loud, *"and the line will keep to the trees until I end it."* She felt him then as she had never felt anything: a Warrior at the very top of the realm, the air ahead gone so dense that her own qi slowed to a crawl, and beside her eight Warriors felt theirs slow with it, and the boards sagged on their arms.

The lamp flames leaned toward him, all five. ***Axefall Decree***. The axe edge came down across the road from the trees to the ditch in a single sweep, and what it cut she could not see. What it did, she saw. The frost peeled off the ruts in one skin and flew. And a weight came down on the row from the end of the blow, the whole width of the road at once, and the boards went down and the soldiers with them, and her knees struck the frozen ground and she got her board up over herself with both arms while the weight pressed the breath out of her ribs bit by bit. Turning her face to breathe, she saw over the fourth wagon's cover the cultivators' cloth tear from its crosspiece on the last pole, rise a moment on the edge of the sweep, and drop into the ditch.

Then the weight was gone. The man stood where he had stood, his shoulders heaving, and he set the butt of the halberd on the ruts and leaned on it for a count of three before he raised his right hand to the height of his shoulder and kept it there. The plain coats went back through the trees the way they had come, two men to each chest and two more with an arm under each of their own fallen, and the man with the lifted hand went last, the haft for a staff, and the broken screen of leaves took him and swung once and was still.

She stood. The board was dented across its middle. Her breath came back in pieces and she let it come, and she straightened in the road with her saber at her side. *"Report by pairs,"* she said, and the voice did not sound like her own, *"and tell me plainly who is hurt."*

*"My pair is whole, Captain,"* Hasler said, as if the news surprised him, *"and I could not tell you how."*

Pair two reported both whole. Ferris reported that his arm would not close on the grip, though he thought the bone sound, and that his pair-mate was winded; the fourth pair's leader reported himself standing and his pair-mate on his hands and knees. Three hurt and none dead, from a man who could have left all nine of them in the road; she said it again to herself, because the first telling had not gone in.

She sent Hasler's pair to make stretchers and to carry water to the man calling under the fourth wagon, and went up the file, counting. The clerk lay across the road with his arms over his face, breathing, and a second driver sat curled round a shoulder beside him. At the lead wheel a driver lay on his back; a guard lay along the trees with his face in the ruts, and another by the lead team with his spear still in his hand. Three. A guard with her arm bound above the elbow sat against the near wheel, tied off by a man who knelt over her. *"Twist it tighter until the bleeding slows,"* Tregear told them both.

The man kneeling over her did not look up from the strap. *"The one by the lead team is Davin Tarn,"* he said. *"His heel never stopped the whole way down from the mine, and I told him to give it a rest... Wil Harker is along the trees, and Edric Gower drove the lead wagon and was talking to his horses when they took him. The man calling under the fourth wagon is Emmet Wicker, who drove that team. I am Gareth Sefton, Captain, and I stood with Ansel Roke at the front, and he is alive at the far wheel, so please send the stretchers there before anything else."*

Roke sat against the far wheel with his side laid open from the ribs to the hip and his shirt black and wet, his saber still in his right hand because nobody had told the hand to let it go. She knelt to him and read him close: ***Warrior Level 1 Mid***, with the qi in him low and thin, flickering like a lamp left to burn on the last of its oil. He looked up and counted the pairs behind her, and then he gave her what he had. *"There were about twenty of them... every one a Warrior by the pressure of him, though I could not give you their Levels exactly. They had halberds and plain coats, with no colour anywhere. They were in the trees before the file came down to the narrow, keeping their qi in so that I felt nothing, and I counted the birds and did nothing with the number."*

*"I have every word of it, and I will carry it to the post exactly as you gave it,"* Tregear said, *"so lie back now and let them lift you."* The hurt were carried up the road to the patrol's post, the clerk first and Roke last, because he would not be lifted until he had said it all.

Back along the file the cloth with the cultivators' sigil lay in the ditch with its face to the frost, and the cloth with the makers' sigil still hung from the crosspiece on the last wagon's pole. One banner. Every child in the kingdom knew that custom before it knew its letters: one cloth hung at a door meant a death in the house. She had seen it on doors all her life, and never on a road.

*"They waited in the trees, and they knew the hour we would come,"* Sefton said behind her. *"I cannot think how anyone could have known it."*

She had no answer. She picked a rag of sacking off the road and lifted it to the nearest lamp. No stamp. No thread of colour. Nothing a kingdom had put there.

*Year 1247, Ilovany, Nuvil, Day 2*

Every time the yard door opened the cold came in with a smell of wet horse, and the hall, which was heated for the sake of its ink and wax and paper, took it a few paces in and no farther. Valia read the queue along the wall before she reached it, the worst-hurt first: a porter standing on one leg, a woman breathing through her mouth, a man nursing a wrist, two yard workers with fingers gone white to the knuckle.
