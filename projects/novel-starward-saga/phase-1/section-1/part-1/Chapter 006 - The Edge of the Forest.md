*Year 1247, Melorany, Orvil, Day 15*

The last log of the day came down out of the trees on five shoulders, and Hercules had the butt of it, where the trunk was thickest and the bark still wet with sap. Selyf Chenoweth and three of his men carried the thin end. Hercules planted his boots in the trodden mud at the head of the slide and settled the load across his shoulder and the crook of his arm, and he felt his back open out under it, easier than it had been all day. A strong man could carry this, and he carried it as a strong man would, and no more.

Below him the slide ran off down the slope, a long trough of planks gone black from years of timber, to the grey roof of Longsaw Mill. The timber track climbed beside it to the high ground. Past the track the hedge ran away along the field strips, and past the hedge the forest stood up out of the dusk, so tall that the dark had already taken the lower half of every trunk. Along the nearest of them the pale lines showed, faint threads in the ridges of the bark.

The log went in on one shove from all five of them, hung at the lip, and ran. Its rumble went down the planks toward the mill and left a smell of cut wood behind it.

*"That makes fourteen for the day,"* Selyf said, wiping his palms down his thighs so that the resin on them dragged at the cloth. *"Jory Michell will have the lot of them eaten before the lamps are lit. He sends a boy up every morning to ask whether we have all gone to sleep, and every morning I tell the boy that trees do not grow faster for being shouted at."*

Hercules counted the crew: Selyf and five men, and an old man on the bench beside the head of the slide. His sense went over the seven of them and came back with breath and sweat and sap and tired muscle in each, and in Selyf alone, thin and hard under the ribs, a thread of qi that made him ***Senior Level 4 Mid***. In the other six there was nothing at all.

Up the timber track, where it came out onto the high ground, Bison stood with his face to the last of the wind and his long coat shut to the throat, as he stood on most evenings. Hercules did not have to look for him. His sense found him the way a hand finds a doorpost in the dark: ***Champion Level 8 High***, kept small, and under it the steady push that could be felt and not measured.

The old man on the bench was looking at neither of them. Austol Bawden sat with his stick between his knees, an old man who had cut timber on this slope for most of his life and now came only to hear it done, and he was looking along the end of the cleared path at the three red posts and the *Thornweft* curtain of barbs behind them. He had been looking for some time.

Hercules saw the birds next. At this hour the first trees should have been loud with small birds going in to roost and quarrelling over the branches, and tonight there were none in the trees at all. They sat along the top of the hedge, packed shoulder to shoulder and silent, and every one of them was turned away from the forest and toward the roofs.

Yestin Hocking came out of the trees at the plank over the ditch, walking fast for a man who never hurried, and he came through the gap in the hedge without stopping there. He carried his bow unstrung across his back and kept looking behind him. Hercules read him as he came, ***Senior Level 6 Mid***, the strongest qi in the village if Hercules and Bison were not counted.

*"The edge has no game,"* Yestin said. He stopped beside the slide and looked once at the birds on the hedge and then did not look at them again. *"I have walked two hours of it, from the far corner to here, and I have not found a print fresher than yesterday's, nor a dropping still warm, nor one bird that would answer me. I would sooner your crew went down to the mill now than finished the stack."*

*"Down to the mill before dark would cost us nothing,"* Selyf said, *"but I would like to know what I am running from before I tell six men to leave a stack that Jory is waiting on, and..."*

*"I have not heard the forest this quiet in my life,"* Austol Bawden said, without turning from the red posts, *"and I have listened to it a long time."*

Selyf stopped with his mouth still open on the next word. Nobody answered the old man. Along the hedge the birds sat on, and not one of them stirred.

Across the ditch, under the first trees, the pigs broke.

They came out in a squealing flood of bristle and trotters, over the ditch and through the plank gap and in among the strips, and the swineherd's girl came after them with a hazel switch in her fist, stripping its leaves with her fingers as she ran, calling in the high coaxing voice she kept for pigs and for nobody else. *"Come back, all of you, come back, come back!"* The coaxing went out of it by the third call. Tegen Angwin was fourteen, and she stopped at the gap with the name of the sow that had led them still in her mouth, and stared after the sow, and did not once look behind her.

Hercules's shoulder was empty now. He did what he had not done in a year, and sent his sense out into the forest.

In that year he had turned it on people and on nothing else, and the village had given back the same plain answer every time, breath and warmth and work, and now and then a thread of qi no thicker than a hair. He held himself in three sizes, and they stood in order in his mind. The first was the one the village knew, a strong man's share. The second was the most he could let out, ***Champion Level 8 High***, a rank that would make a stranger set his feet as for a blow. Under that lay the third, which pushed and had no name he could give it, with a weight laid over it that had not shifted in a year. The sense belonged to the second size, and he let it go out as thin as the first.

It came back full.

Between the trunks, a long way along the edge toward the crossing, bodies were moving in the dark, many of them, low and heavy and fast, and all moving together. He read them the way he read a load, a range at a time. The lean ones in front ran from the middle of the second Level to the high end of the fourth. The bulk behind them, slow and straight, ran from the middle of the fourth to the high end of the sixth. Higher up, above the treetops, others were gliding in on the third and the fifth. Every one of them was a Warrior by the pressure of it, there were more than twenty, and none of them was a match for him. He set that aside at once. The roofs below were no match for any of them.

A hare burst through the hedge at the plank gap and ran between his boots without swerving. A doe followed it, and another, and a stag with his head up and the whites of his eyes showing, and they went down the slope toward the roofs and took no notice of the men at all. Then the hedge shook along half its length, and the host came out of the dark.

The wolves came first, across the strips in pairs, one running wide and one close, lean and dark grey as the hour and pale at the throat, as high as a man's hip. Gloam Wolves, the hunters of the deep edge at dusk, and in a year of Yestin's talk at the slide Hercules had never once heard of them running with anything but their own kind. Behind them the hedge bulged and split and three bears came through it as a man goes through a door, crown first, heavy red-brown bodies with a branching bony crown on each brow like a stag's antlers, and the drumming of them came up through his boot soles. Hart Bears, the village called them, for the crown, and they put a fence down with their heads before they put anything else down at all. And above the strips and the roofs, low in the last light, came the leather-winged gliders, as wide as a man's arms can spread, with a hooked claw at each wing joint to rake thatch for a roost. Hook Wings.

*"Wolves and Hart Bears together!"* Yestin shouted, and his voice cracked on it. *"Run for the roofs, all of you on the strips, run now, before it reaches you!"*

The workers on the strips needed no telling. A dozen of them, every one empty to his sense, had been finishing the last rows with hoes in the half-light, and they dropped the hoes and ran, scattering every way. The host went past them. It did not slow for one. A wolf's flank brushed a woman's skirt and she screamed and ran on, unhurt, and the rest went by her down the slope toward the first houses with no more notice of the people than of the stubble.

It had come out of the trees a long way along the edge, down toward the crossing, and the slide and the high ground lay behind it. By the time Hercules understood what he was reading, it was across the strips and in among the nearest houses, and he stood at the head of the slide with an empty shoulder and the whole slope between him and it.

The girl had come to his side without his seeing her and taken hold of his sleeve. There was nothing in her for his sense to read but a fast pulse in the wrist that gripped him. *"Go down with Selyf's men and keep in the middle of them,"* he told her, *"and do not stop for the pigs."* She let go, and went.

He ran. He ran as a heavy man runs, which is not fast, shortening each stride to keep his body over his feet, down the timber track and onto the lane to the roofs. The heat rose in him as the run went on. It came up from no place he could point to, and the weight settled over it, flat and even, and between the two there was no room to run any faster than a strong man runs. He let nothing out. He counted instead: twenty-eight, and not one of them running from anything.

Behind him Selyf's crew caught up their axes and came after him, boots loud on the planks. High on the right a long coat flared as Bison went off the far side of the high ground, away from the track and toward the roofs, and the slope took him out of sight.

The hall bell began. It was struck hard and fast, by someone who knew exactly how hard a bell could be struck, and Selyf looked up as he ran. *"That is the Chief on the rope,"* he said, *"and nobody else strikes it like that."* Hercules's sense ran ahead of him down the lane and found her at the hall door, Morwenna Keast, the Village Chief, ***Senior Level 3 Low***, with both fists on the rope and her whole back behind every stroke. Then, over the bell, from the crossing, a woman screamed.

The Hook Wings had come down on the near thatch in a row and were tearing it up by the armful with the hooks at their wing joints. Over the inn the Wood Ravens stooped, black birds the size of hounds with iron-dark beaks, birds that go for whoever carries a lamp and stab at lamps and thatch and shutters when no one does. His sense took them in as he ran, from the low end of the second Level to the middle of the fourth, and went on.

The long pole of the toll bar at Alder Bridge was snapped across the road, and the short end hung from its post. A red-brown rump was leaving the forge through its back wall in a spray of brick, and at Rowantree Inn a second bear had gone through the stable doors. Something pale slid in under the bakery's front door like water under a sill, and another after it, and a third went up the wall to the eaves: Bone Foxes, no bigger than dogs, the colour of old bone, from the bottom of the first Level to the middle of the second. They came in under doors and along beams after whatever was kept in the house.

People ran every way, toward the hall with children across their shoulders, away from it for a child who was not there. His sense went over the press of them in one sweep and came back with breath and fear and nothing under either: all of them unawakened. He ran toward what was hurt. The heat climbed in him with the run, and the old weight pressed it down, and his pace stayed a heavy man's.

The forge had lost its front. A wolf had the smith's knee. It stood with its feet braced on his apron, pulling.

Hercules took the wolf first, because it was the load that moved. One hand in the loose skin of its neck and one in the fur of its flank brought it up off the knee with a strip of apron in its jaws, and he swung it out through the hole and let go. The beam came second. One hand under it, and it did not stir. Both hands, and it rose the width of two fingers. A second scream, from the inn, broke off in the middle of a note. It was Jenifer Pender's. Jory Michell was running down the lane from the mill at the head of nine mill men with axes, a short wide man with sawdust in the creases of his neck, and in him Hercules's sense found a thin thread of qi, ***Senior Level 2 Low***. *Year 1247, Melorany, Sanvil, Day 20*

The room was nine strides long and five wide, and he had walked it on the first night and had not needed to since. He drew his right hand out of its sleeve and laid the palm flat on the table, and the wood was as cold as the floor.

The air above the table lit. A wide pane of pale light stood in it, upright, as wide as a door and clear as water in a glass, the colour of a candle seen through paper, with no frame and no edge but its own. It made no sound. It threw no shadow on the stone. Beyond it lay a haze, like breath on a cold window, and in the haze a shape, motionless, which had been there before the light came and did not greet him. He had worked the pane two hundred times and had never learned what held it up, and he had stopped asking in the first week. The shape had never once been late. The favour was the only coin that this room paid in. Season by season he had put it away, and at the far end of the seasons there was the realm above Ruler, and no road went to it except through this light. Ninety. A hundred. The smoke still leaned toward the door. He did not shift his feet. Somewhere under the stone a door closed, a long way down, and the sound reached him as a change in the cold and not as a sound.

*"You will go on,"* the voice said.

The tone came first, as it always did, and it was the tone he knew: even and unhurried, with no word leaning on another, and it did not rise at the end. *"What is wanted is chaos and pressure, kept up, and neither is measured by any one village, so the loss of this one changes nothing that was asked. The task is the same as before, and the method remains yours."*

The pane folded on the last word. It closed from its edges inward, like a page being shut, and was gone, and the haze and the shape went with it, and the pale light went off the stone, and there was the table and the lamp and the cold. For a time the pane stood on the inside of his eyes, a pale door in the dark, and then that went too.

His palm came up off the wood and left a damp print that faded while he watched. The left hand was already in its sleeve, and the right followed it, and both went deep, to the elbow, and held each other there. His breath went out of him in one long thread, and the lamp flame bent to it, and he stood motionless until the flame stood straight again. He did not sit. The coat hung on the chair, and he did not put it on. It was a long road to the throne above the thrones, and he had been told to go on along it. The cold came up through the soles of his feet, and he let it. He went on standing as he had stood, with his head still bowed, until the lamp had burned a good way down. Then, under his breath, he recited what was owed and by whom, in order. A count of the two men, owed by him, not yet begun. A second matter, owed by him, to be opened when the method was chosen. The favour, owed to the voice, drawn on and never paid down. He came to the end and began again from the top, and the list was one item longer than it had been at dusk.
