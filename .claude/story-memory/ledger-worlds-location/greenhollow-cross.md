The forest wall rises at the back of the village, and the farmland spreads out in front of it. On Day 17 (first light into the evening) the village is seen mending: the Hedge Well at morning with a dark wet rope, the doors rehung, the bar of Alder Bridge mended, a length of the saw shed's wall raised, the burn pit smoking past the last roofs, a noon meeting in the mill square, and, toward evening, the three bitten sitting in the bakery doorway and the old man on the bench by the inn (§5.8, §5.12).

### 5.2 — Fields and Farming (author-authored, 2026-10-06)

- Wheat, barley and rye grow in the cleared fields just beyond the tree line.
- Root crops and beans grow in the kitchen plots behind every house.

### 5.3 — Forest Work (author-authored, 2026-10-06)

- Woodcutters fell and trim timber, working the forest in rotation so it regrows.
- Sawyers cut logs into planks and beams at the sawpit and the sawmill.
- Charcoal burners tend slow kilns in the clearings and supply fuel to the forge.
- Hunters and trappers bring in game and pelts.
- Foresters mark which trees may be cut and replant what is felled.
- Log slides and a timber track carry felled logs from the forest down to the mill yard.

### 5.4 — Mills and Machines (author-authored, 2026-10-06; technology profile)

- The waterwheel grist mill grinds the village's grain, and nearby hamlets pay a share of their grain to use it.
- The water-powered sawmill drives a saw blade, so planks cost a fraction of what hand-sawing does.
- A small oil press works flax and rapeseed from the same stream.
- A lathe and a trip-hammer, both turned by the stream, serve the carpenter and the smith.
- Pulley cranes move heavy timber in the yard.

### 5.7 — Daily Life (author-authored, 2026-10-06; population)

- About two hundred households live here, along with an inn and stables, a small shrine and a meeting hall where disputes are settled.

### 5.9 — Distinctive Sensory Signature (axis 12)

A visitor at the top of the village slope at first light (1247.1.1 / PD 1; Ref: 1.1.1.002) meets the cold first, with frost white along the length of the well rope, and a white light with no gold in it, white as a peeled rod, that has reached the treetops and nothing below them until the sun comes down the trunks; a thin wind comes down along the hedge from the forest. The sounds are small and near: a bucket knocking once on the wall of the well, a rope let down a pull at a time, boots quick and flat on the ruts, the grass hissing beside a fence. Below the slope the roofs lie low and close together, every shutter shut and one chimney smoking. Behind the hedge and the ditch the forest wall rises (cross-ref: skyvein-forest.md §5.9).

At the crossing a little later the sounds are those of waking work: a pencil squeaking on a slate, stable doors dragged back against a wall, the waterwheel of Longsaw Mill taking the stream with a long wet groan and then its saw, a thin note that climbs and falls and holds at the top, and a cart drumming onto the planks of Alder Bridge. The smells are new bread from the bakery door and warm straw from the inn yard, and the sun finds the walls so that sleeves go up. In the mill yard the next day the sounds are the waterwheel thudding behind the wall of the grist mill, the saw whining through the fence, a sack dropped on the scale and seed pouring in a dry brown stream, with the talk of the night's frost and of the sowing; in the lane there is shouting, and the saw goes on far off with its one note. On the farmland on the third day the ground is dry on top and wet below, the wind comes steady out of the trees smelling of moss, pigs grunt across the ditch, and an axe rings among the trunks from no side a man could name (cross-ref: skyvein-forest.md §5.9).

At dusk on 1247.2.15 / PD 43 at the head of the log slide (Ref: 1.1.1.006) the sounds are a log rumbling down the planks toward the mill and boots in trodden mud, and the smells are cut wood, resin and sap; the forest stands "so tall that the dark had already taken the lower half of every trunk", with the pale lines faint threads in the ridges of the bark, and it is silent: no small bird in the trees, and Austol Bawden says "I have not heard the forest this quiet in my life". In the lanes during the rush the sounds are the hall bell struck hard, screams, the drumming of the bears' charge coming up through boot soles and thatch torn up by the armful, and the smell is the evening bake (hot bread and scorched flour) with "something musky and wild" under it; in the bakery yard the oven's glow lays shadows long across the earth. In the hall the air smells of wet wool, lamp oil and fear gone cold, with new bread through it. Outside, two hours later, the air is cold, steady and moving, and carries "the smell of cold water on stones" from the far side of the hills. On 1247.2.17 / PD 45 the morning smells of old smoke, a thread of smoke stands up from the burn pit, and the sounds of the mending are a mallet whose blows are counted aloud, a plane, nails and a chisel.

**Narrative summary (as of Chapter 002).** Greenhollow Cross is a village of about two hundred households at the very edge of a great forest, where the last trees give way to open fields. A little later the crossing wakes in the sun: the toll collector, Pasco Cobb, pulls down the short end of the bar of Alder Bridge, a bridge of grey planks on stone legs, to raise the long pole clear of the road for a charcoal cart, and counts the load onto a slate that hangs from the post; the waterwheel of Longsaw Mill takes the stream and its saw starts; the stable doors of Rowantree Inn are dragged back; the road fills with people and their loads; Hercules lifts the cooper's full cask onto his cart in the inn yard; and Bison stands on the bare bank at the far end of the bridge in the wind off the forest.
