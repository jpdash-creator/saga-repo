Dace caught the boat hook in both hands as Teague swung it across the quay, and the two men stood locked with the pole between them, boots skidding on the wet stone.

The reason the lock was so dangerous lay in the leverage of the hook itself. A boat hook is a long pole with an iron claw at one end, and a man who grips it near the claw gives up the strength that a man at the far end gains, because the pole turns about the point where the hands meet. A heavy man at the long end can lift a lighter man clean off his feet with no great effort, which is why dockhands are taught to take the middle.

The principle behind the turn is the one that governs a gate on its hinge. The farther a push lands from the pivot, the less force it needs, and the nearer it lands, the more it takes. Teague, being the taller, held the long end, and the exchange had been decided before either man moved.

Dace twisted the pole, and Teague went over the bollard and into the water.
