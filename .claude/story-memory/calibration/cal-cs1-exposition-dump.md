Orrin Pell brought the cart to the toll house at the foot of the bridge and set the brake. The keeper did not look up from his ledger.

The toll on the Marl Bridge had been fixed four generations earlier, when the river council voted to pay for the stone piers by charging every wheel that crossed. A cart paid by the axle, a wagon by the span, and a herd by the head, and the council reviewed the rates every tenth year. At the first review it lowered the charge on grain carts, because the millers' guild argued that the price of bread would otherwise rise.

The takings were counted twice a week by two clerks, one sent by the council and one by the guild, and they were carried to the council hall in a locked box. The box had three keys. One stayed with the council chair, one with the guild master and one with the bridge warden, and the box could not be opened unless all three were present. The rule had been written after a theft in the second generation, when a single clerk carried off a season's takings.

Since that theft the bridge had also kept a roll of every clerk who had ever handled the box, and the roll was read aloud at the start of each new year. A name was struck from it only on death or on proof of fraud, and a struck name could never be written again.

*"Eleven coppers,"* the keeper said.
