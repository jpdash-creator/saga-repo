## Overview

This file is a **factual reference resource about real-world armies, navies and air forces.** It exists so that a writer, planner, or auditor working on a Starward Saga military scene has one place to check *scale* — how big a company really is, what rank actually commands a battalion, how many platoons go into one — instead of guessing or half-remembering.

**What this file is not, stated plainly and up front:**

- It is **not a canon Part.** It has no Part number, it is not one of the forty consolidated canon skills enumerated in `.claude/guardrails/authority/guardrail-consolidated-skill-reading.md`, and its existence does not change that count. Its name deliberately sits in the `reference-` group, which no canon Part uses, so that it can never be mistaken for one, and it must never be cited as canon.
- It is **not authority.** Where a canon skill, a ledger entry, an established prose fact, or an author ruling says something different from this file, **the canon source governs absolutely and this file is simply irrelevant to the question.** Part 22 Area 5.4 already carries the canon command-chain archetype for Starward Saga military factions; that archetype governs, not the tables below.
- It is **not a naming mandate.** Nothing here obliges the saga to use the word "battalion" or the rank "Colonel." Part 28's Naming Freedom Law and the author's rulings decide what the saga's units and ranks are called. This file supplies the *yardstick*, not the *name*.
- It is **not a source of established canon facts.** No audit finding, ledger entry, or continuity claim may cite this file as evidence that anything is true in the story.

**What it is for.** Three jobs. First, **sizing** — when a scene says a formation moved, this tells you roughly how many people that ought to be and who ought to be at the front of them. Second, **rank fit** — when a character commands something, this tells you whether the rank and the command are plausibly matched, and what the senior enlisted figure standing beside them would be. Third, **error avoidance** — the US/UK divergences in Block R9 are the traps that make military fiction read as unresearched, and they are the reason the author asked for this file to distinguish the two national practices rather than blend them.

**How this file is divided.** The numbered headings below are **Reference Blocks, R1 through R10.** They are **not canon Areas.** An `R`-number never appears in a canon citation, never resolves to a rule, and never carries the weight that `§Area N` carries in a consolidated canon skill. The blocks are: R1 scope and how to read the numbers; R2 the general organisational principles; R3 the land ladder smallest to largest; R4 the US Army rank ladder; R5 the British Army rank ladder; R6 naval organisation and ranks (USN and RN); R7 air organisation and ranks (USAF, USSF and RAF); R8 the cross-service and cross-national equivalence table; R9 the common errors and genuine divergences; and R10 the **Starward Saga mapping rules and pointers to the ledgers** (the figures themselves live in the ledgers).

## R1 — Scope, and How to Read the Numbers

### R1.1 — Two nations, not an average

Where US and UK practice genuinely differ, **this file states both and never averages them.** The two armies are not variants of one system; they diverge on where the officer/NCO boundary falls, on what a "regiment" is, on which ranks command which formations, and on what a warrant officer even is. Blending them produces a force that reads as neither, which is precisely the failure mode this file exists to prevent.

### R1.2 — Strength figures are nominal establishment, not headcount

Every strength figure below is a **doctrinal or establishment figure** — the number the organisation is *designed* to hold. Real units are almost never at that number. Four things drive the variance, and a writer who wants a unit under or over strength should pick one of them and say so:

1. **Manning shortfall.** Peacetime units routinely run at 80–95% of establishment; a unit on a long or unpopular posting can run far lower.
2. **Attrition in the field.** Casualties, sickness, and detachments cut a formation's fighting strength well below its paper strength within weeks of contact. Twentieth-century infantry battalions frequently fought at half establishment for extended periods.
3. **Branch.** An armoured, artillery, engineer, signals or logistics unit at the same rung as an infantry unit will have a different headcount, because the establishment is built around equipment crews rather than rifles. An armoured battalion-equivalent is smaller in people and vastly larger in tonnage.
4. **Era.** Twentieth-century formations were much larger than their modern namesakes. A First World War division ran 15,000–25,000; a Second World War US infantry division about 14,000–16,000; a modern division's *combat* element is smaller still while its support tail is proportionally larger. Figures below are modern unless flagged.

### R1.3 — The teeth-to-tail caveat

At every rung above company, a substantial and increasing share of the headcount is **not** combat troops: signallers, medics, engineers, quartermasters, mechanics, drivers, clerks, intelligence staff, cooks. A brigade of 4,500 does not put 4,500 rifles on a line. For a formation of brigade size and above, a rough working assumption is that the manoeuvre element is a minority of the total. Fiction that treats a division's paper strength as its assault strength is overstating by a large factor.

### R1.4 — Provenance of the content in this file

Everything in Blocks R1 through R9 is **real-world public reference material** — open-source doctrinal organisation and published rank structures. It is neither `[ASKED]` nor `[DERIVED]` in the Part 39 provenance sense, because those tags describe how a *canon* fact was obtained and nothing in R1–R9 is a canon fact. Block R10 is the only part of this file that touches the saga.

## R2 — General Principles Every Writer Needs

These are the load-bearing ideas. The tables in R3–R7 are just these principles worked out in detail; where a table and a principle seem to disagree, the principle is the thing to reason from.

### R2.1 — Span of command: the rule of three to five

**A commander at any rung directly controls between three and five subordinate elements.** Not one, not ten. This is the single organising fact from which the whole ladder falls out, and it is why the ladder has the number of rungs it has rather than more or fewer.

The reason is human, not doctrinal. Under stress, with imperfect information, in noise, one person can hold three to five moving things in their head — brief them, track them, react to them, and commit a reserve. Below three, the commander cannot manoeuvre: you cannot fix with one element and flank with another if you have only two, and with two you have no reserve at all. Above five or six, the commander loses the thread: briefing takes too long, reports arrive faster than they can be processed, and control degrades into a list of units that are merely present.

The historical shorthand is the **rule of three**, or the **triangular organisation** — three manoeuvre subordinates plus a support element and a headquarters. Three is the classic minimum for fire-and-manoeuvre with a reserve: **one fixes, one flanks, one is held back.** Four and five appear where the formation also needs a dedicated weapons or reconnaissance element. The older **square organisation** (four manoeuvre subordinates, used by First World War divisions) was abandoned precisely because it proved unwieldy for a single commander.

For a fictional force the practical consequence is: **decide the smallest unit, then multiply by three to five at each rung, and you will land on a plausible ladder.** A structure where every commander has nine or ten direct subordinates is not a bigger army — it is an army that does not work, and it reads that way.

### R2.2 — Why NCO command stops at around platoon level

Across both the US and UK systems, non-commissioned officers command **fire teams, squads/sections, and — as second-in-command — platoons.** Command of a platoon and everything above it belongs to commissioned officers. The line is not arbitrary; it falls at a real change in what commanding means.

**Below the line, command is direct and physical.** A section commander can *see* every one of their eight soldiers, can control them by voice and hand signal, and can physically go and correct a soldier who is doing the wrong thing. The commander is inside the fight, firing their own weapon, and the unit occupies one piece of ground. The skill required is mastery of the immediate — battle drills, weapon handling, the ground in front of you — and it is learned by doing it for years, which is exactly what an NCO's career is.

**Above the line, command becomes abstract.** A company commander cannot see all three platoons at once. They command by radio, by map, by a plan made in advance, and by decisions taken on reports they cannot personally verify. They allocate resources, coordinate with adjacent units and supporting arms, task-organise, and answer for a piece of ground rather than a piece of the firing line. The skill required is planning and judgement under uncertainty, which is what officer training is for.

**And there is a legal half to it.** A commission is a formal grant of authority from the head of state — the President in the US, the Sovereign in the UK. It carries the delegated power to give lawful orders that put lives at risk, the legal responsibility for how force is used, and disciplinary authority. NCO rank is an appointment *within* the enlisted body; it carries enormous practical authority and comparatively little of that formal sovereign delegation. The commission is the mechanism by which the state's power to kill is passed down the chain, and it is passed down at the level where a leader begins making decisions the state must answer for.

Platoon level is where the two meet, and the meeting is deliberate: a platoon has a commissioned officer commanding it — usually the most junior officer in the army, in their first job — paired with an experienced senior NCO as second-in-command (the US Platoon Sergeant, the UK Platoon Sergeant). This pairing of an inexperienced officer with a veteran NCO is universal in both armies, and it is one of the most reliably dramatic structures in real military life.

**For fiction:** an NCO commanding a company-sized force is not impossible — it happens when the officers are dead, and it is always marked as an emergency — but a force whose *normal* structure puts NCOs in charge of large formations has, in effect, abolished the officer corps. That is a substantial worldbuilding claim rather than a detail, and it should be made on purpose.

### R2.3 — Administrative versus tactical formation

**This is the distinction fiction gets wrong most often, and it is the key to understanding the word "regiment."**

A **tactical formation** is a body that is commanded and that fights as an entity. It has a commander with a headquarters, receives orders, manoeuvres, and its subordinate units go where it goes. A battalion, a brigade and a division are tactical formations.

An **administrative formation** exists for recruiting, training, records, career management, tradition and identity. It never appears on a battlefield as a body, has no field commander in the tactical sense, and its constituent units may be scattered across the world under entirely different tactical chains of command. The British infantry regiment is the purest example: a soldier belongs to the regiment for life, wears its cap badge, is recruited by it and returns to it — but fights inside a battalion that sits under a brigade which has nothing to do with the regiment.

Some bodies are **both**. A US Brigade Combat Team is a tactical formation and also the unit around which soldiers' careers are managed. A British armoured regiment is simultaneously a tactical unit of roughly 500 and a permanent identity-holding body with its own history and traditions.

**Why it matters for writing:** if a force has an administrative layer, then a character's stated affiliation ("she is of the such-and-such") and a character's operational chain ("she reports to whoever holds this valley") are two different facts that can pull against each other. That is a rich source of loyalty conflict — and an extremely easy source of accidental continuity error if the writer has not decided which kind of body they invented.

### R2.4 — Rank versus appointment

**Rank** is what a person *holds*: a substantive grade, attached to pay, seniority and the formal hierarchy, held whether or not they are currently employed in a particular job. **Appointment** — US usage often says "billet" or "position" — is what a person *does*: the job they currently occupy.

The two are related but distinct, and both systems are full of places where the appointment has its own title that is not a rank:

- **Ship's captain.** The commanding officer of any naval vessel is addressed and referred to as "the Captain" regardless of actual rank. A Royal Navy minehunter may be commanded by a Lieutenant; aboard, he is the Captain. Meanwhile **Captain** is also a substantive naval rank (OF-5, equivalent to an army Colonel). A single scene can contain a Captain-by-rank and a captain-by-appointment who are not the same person and are four ranks apart.
- **Commodore, US Navy.** Not a substantive rank in current US service — it is the *appointment* title of an officer, usually a Captain (O-6), commanding a squadron of ships or a group of units. In the Royal Navy, **Commodore is a substantive rank** (OF-6). Same word, two different categories, two navies.
- **First Sergeant / Command Sergeant Major (US); Company Sergeant Major / Regimental Sergeant Major (UK).** These are the senior-enlisted appointments at company and battalion level. In US usage, First Sergeant and Command Sergeant Major are treated as ranks in their own right at E-8 and E-9. In UK usage, CSM and RSM are **appointments** held by warrant officers of Class 2 and Class 1 respectively — so a British RSM's *rank* is Warrant Officer Class 1, and "RSM" is his job.
- **Colonel of the Regiment (UK).** A ceremonial appointment as the figurehead of a British regiment, frequently held by a serving or retired general officer or a member of the royal family. It is not the rank of Colonel and it involves no command.
- **Acting, local, temporary and brevet rank.** A person may hold a higher rank for as long as they hold a particular job, reverting afterwards. This is common in wartime expansion, and it is where the phrase "acting Major" comes from.

**For fiction:** the rank/appointment split is the mechanism that lets a character hold real authority their rank alone would not explain, and the mechanism that lets a character be outranked by someone they nonetheless direct. Both are useful; both need to be deliberate rather than accidental.

### R2.5 — Arm-of-service naming variants

The *same rung* of the ladder is called different things depending on the branch. This is not decoration — it tells a reader instantly what kind of unit they are looking at. Both nations do it, with broadly the same vocabulary.

| Ladder rung | Infantry | Cavalry / Armour | Artillery | Engineers, Signals, Logistics (UK) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Battalion-equivalent | **Battalion** | **Regiment** (UK) / **Squadron** or **Battalion** (US) | **Regiment** (UK) / **Battalion** (US) | **Regiment** (UK) |
| Company-equivalent | **Company** | **Squadron** (UK) / **Troop** (US cavalry) | **Battery** | **Squadron** |
| Platoon-equivalent | **Platoon** | **Troop** | **Troop** (gun troop) / **Section** (two guns) | **Troop** |

Note the collisions this creates. All of them are real, and all of them are traps:

- **"Troop"** is a platoon-sized armoured or cavalry unit of three to four vehicles — but "troops" in the plural means soldiers in general. A US cavalry **troop** is *company*-sized while a UK armoured **troop** is *platoon*-sized: the word names two different rungs in the two armies.
- **"Squadron"** is a company-sized armoured unit in the UK, a flying unit of squadron status in the RAF and USAF, and a group of *ships* in both navies. Several different sizes, one word.
- **"Section"** is a squad in the British Army (eight soldiers), a two-gun sub-unit in the artillery, and in the US Army usually a two-team element or a staff subdivision — while in the US Navy a section is a watch-keeping subdivision of a crew.
- **"Battery"** always means artillery and is always company-equivalent. This one is stable in both nations.

**For fiction:** giving different branches different names for the same rung is a cheap and extremely effective way to make a military feel institutionally real, because it signals that the organisation grew rather than being designed in one sitting. It also demands a glossary, or readers lose the scale.

### R2.6 — Command chain versus staff

A formation's headquarters contains two different kinds of people, and confusing them is a classic error.

**The chain of command** is the line of commanders. Each one commands, gives orders, and is answerable. It runs one person deep at each rung.

**The staff** are officers and NCOs who plan, advise and administer *on the commander's behalf.* They do not command anything. Their instructions carry the commander's authority, never their own. Both nations number the staff branches identically:

| Branch | Function |
|---|---|
| **1** | Personnel, manning, casualties, discipline |
| **2** | Intelligence, enemy assessment |
| **3** | Operations — the current fight, and training |
| **4** | Logistics, supply, transport, maintenance |
| **5** | Plans (future operations) |
| **6** | Signals, communications, information systems |
| **7–9** | Where used: training, resources and finance, civil-military cooperation |

The prefix letter tells you the level: **S** at battalion and brigade (S-3 = operations officer), **G** at division and above (G-2 = intelligence), **N** in naval commands (N-4 = fleet logistics), **A** in air commands, and **J** in a joint headquarters combining services (J-5 = joint plans).

Ranks attach predictably: a battalion's S-3 is typically a Major; a division's G-3 a Colonel. A staff appointment is normally a step *between* command appointments in an officer's career — command a company, serve on a battalion staff, command a battalion, serve on a brigade or division staff, and so on. In the British Army this is especially pronounced, because **Colonel is essentially a staff rank** (see R5.3).

**Second-in-command** sits between the two categories: a real deputy who assumes command if the commander falls, and who otherwise runs the headquarters. UK usage says **2IC**; US usage says **Executive Officer (XO)**.

### R2.7 — Task organisation: what actually deploys

Formations on paper are rarely what deploys. Units are **cross-attached** for a mission: an armoured regiment lends a squadron to an infantry battalion and borrows a rifle company in exchange, engineers and artillery observers are attached, and the resulting mixed force operates under one commander for the duration.

- **UK: battlegroup.** A task-organised, battalion-sized, combined-arms force under a Lieutenant Colonel. Its company-sized equivalent is a **combat team**; its platoon-sized equivalent a **combat group**.
- **US: task force.** Battalion-sized and task-organised, usually written "TF" plus a name or number. Its company-sized equivalent is a **company team**.

The important structural fact: **from brigade upward, formations are normally permanently combined-arms** — infantry, armour, artillery, engineers, signals, logistics and medical all organic to the formation — **while battalions and below are normally single-arm and receive attachments for a task.** The brigade is therefore the lowest rung that can independently conduct a sustained operation, which is why it has become the basic deployable building block in both armies.

### R3.2 — The rungs in detail

**Fire team (4).** The smallest body with an internal structure rather than a list of people. The US infantry fire team is a Team Leader, a Rifleman, a Grenadier and an Automatic Rifleman — one weapon of each type, so the team can suppress and move without help. The UK fire team is four soldiers of a section, one of them carrying the section's light support weapon. Two fire teams make a squad/section, which is the smallest body that can genuinely fire-and-manoeuvre: one team pins, the other moves. A fire team leader is a junior NCO — a US Sergeant, a UK Lance Corporal — and is the last leader in the chain who is also fully a rifleman.

**Squad (US, 9) and Section (UK, 8).** The basic tactical building block of infantry in both armies, and the *only* body most soldiers ever experience directly. The US rifle squad is a Squad Leader (Staff Sergeant) plus two four-man fire teams. The UK section is a Corporal as commander, a Lance Corporal as second-in-command, and six others, organised as two fire teams — the commander leading one, the 2IC the other. **The rank divergence here is real and worth noting:** the US squad is led by a Staff Sergeant (E-6) while the equivalent UK section is led by a Corporal (OR-4), two rungs lower on the enlisted ladder. The UK Corporal carries at section level a responsibility the US Army places with a more senior NCO.

**Platoon (US ~30–42, UK ~27–36).** Three or four squads/sections plus a small headquarters. The US infantry rifle platoon is three nine-man rifle squads plus a weapons squad (machine-gun and anti-armour teams) and a headquarters of the platoon leader, platoon sergeant, radio operator and medic — approximately 39 to 42 in total. The UK platoon is three eight-man sections plus a headquarters of platoon commander, platoon sergeant, signaller and a small weapons element — approximately 28 to 30. **This is the first rung commanded by a commissioned officer** (see R2.2), and in both armies it is the newest officer's first command, with an experienced NCO as second-in-command.

**Company (US 100–200, UK 100–120).** Three to four platoons plus a company headquarters. **The most important single divergence in the whole ladder lives here: a US company is commanded by a Captain, a British company by a Major.** The British Army places one full rank more seniority at company level than the US Army does, which cascades — a British Captain is typically a company second-in-command or a staff officer, not a company commander. The company is the lowest rung with its own administrative machinery (a company clerk, a stores element, a quartermaster function), and it is the level at which soldiers begin to be numbers on a return rather than faces the commander knows individually. Armoured units call this rung a **squadron**, artillery a **battery**, US cavalry a **troop**.

**Battalion (US 300–1,000, UK 550–750).** Three to six companies. Both armies agree here: a battalion is commanded by a **Lieutenant Colonel**. A typical British infantry battalion holds three rifle companies, a support company (mortars, anti-tank, reconnaissance, machine guns) and a headquarters company, at roughly 600–650 all ranks. A US infantry battalion holds three rifle companies plus a weapons company and a headquarters company. The battalion is the smallest formation with a genuine staff (S-1 through S-6, see R2.6), its own logistics, and the ability to sustain itself in the field for a period without external support — which makes it the classic unit of manoeuvre in fiction as in fact. Its senior enlisted figure is the US Command Sergeant Major or the UK Regimental Sergeant Major, who is the most powerful non-commissioned figure most soldiers will ever deal with.

**Regiment — see R3.3.** This word is the biggest trap in the file and gets its own subsection.

**Brigade (3,000–5,000).** Three to five battalions plus brigade troops — artillery, engineers, signals, reconnaissance, logistics, medical. **The commanding ranks diverge: a US brigade is commanded by a Colonel (O-6), a British brigade by a Brigadier (OF-6), one rung higher.** This is a direct consequence of the company-level divergence propagating up the ladder. The brigade is the lowest permanently combined-arms formation in both armies and therefore the lowest rung that can be sent somewhere and left to get on with it — which is why the modern US Army built its whole force structure around the **Brigade Combat Team** (armoured, infantry, or Stryker variants, roughly 4,400–4,700 each) as the deployable package.

**Division (10,000–20,000).** Three to four brigades plus divisional artillery, aviation, and sustainment elements. Commanded by a **Major General** in both armies — but note that a British Major General is OF-7 while a US Major General is O-8/OF-7 as well, so the two-star division commander is one of the few points where the two systems genuinely line up. A division is a self-contained fighting machine with the depth to sustain an offensive over time rather than an operation over days. Historically it is the formation soldiers name in memoirs, and the rung at which a formation acquires a reputation of its own.

**Corps (40,000–90,000).** Two to five divisions plus corps troops. Commanded by a **Lieutenant General** (three-star). Reference figures vary widely and honestly: US Army sources commonly quote 20,000–45,000 for a corps, but that figure descends from an era of smaller divisions — a corps of three modern divisions plus corps artillery, aviation and sustainment comfortably exceeds 50,000. Use the figure that matches the divisions you have specified rather than a quoted total. The corps is the level at which command becomes primarily operational art — shaping a campaign — rather than fighting a battle. The UK rarely fields a national corps today; its corps-level contribution is the framework headquarters of a multinational NATO corps.

**Field army (100,000–300,000).** Two or more corps. Historically commanded by a full **General** (four-star). In modern US practice a "numbered army" is more often an administrative and theatre-support headquarters than a field-manoeuvre command, and is typically held by a Lieutenant General. Field armies are, in practice, wartime constructs.

**Army group (400,000–1,000,000+).** Two to four field armies, controlling an entire national or coalition front. A Second World War construct with no standing peacetime equivalent in either nation; commanded by a General or, in British practice of the period, a Field Marshal. If a fictional force fields army groups, it is fighting a total war on a continental front, and that is a claim about the scale of the whole setting rather than about one formation.

### R3.3 — "Regiment": the two nations mean two different things

This is worth stating in full, because using the word carelessly is the fastest way to make a military read as unresearched.

**In the British Army, "regiment" means one of two things depending on the branch, and neither is what a US reader expects.**

1. **The infantry regiment is an administrative and identity body, not a tactical formation.** It recruits, holds the cap badge, the battle honours, the traditions, the museum and the regimental association. It contains one or more **battalions**, which are the things that actually fight — and those battalions are dispersed to whichever brigades need them. The regiment has no field commander: its **Colonel of the Regiment** is a ceremonial figurehead, often a serving or retired general, sometimes a member of the royal family. A soldier's regiment is the institution they belong to for life; their battalion is where they work.
2. **In the cavalry and armour, the artillery, the engineers, the signals, the Army Air Corps and the logistic corps, the regiment *is* the battalion-sized tactical unit.** It runs roughly 500–700 personnel, it is commanded by a **Lieutenant Colonel**, it has a Regimental Sergeant Major, and it is subdivided into **squadrons** (armour, engineers, signals) or **batteries** (artillery) rather than companies. An armoured regiment is a battalion by any other name, and calling it a battalion in front of a cavalryman is an error of some social consequence.

**In the modern US Army, the regiment is chiefly a lineage device.** Unit designations of the form "2nd Battalion, 502nd Infantry Regiment" name the regiment as the historical parent whose honours and heritage the battalion carries — but the regiment does not exist as a headquarters, and the battalions bearing one regiment's name may serve in entirely different divisions. A handful of genuine regiments survive as real, commanded formations — armoured cavalry regiments, the 75th Ranger Regiment, the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment, the 3rd US Infantry Regiment ("The Old Guard") — and where a real US regiment exists it runs roughly 1,500–3,000 and is commanded by a **Colonel**. The US Marine Corps, by contrast, fields regiments as genuine tactical formations throughout its divisions.

**The practical rule for a writer:** decide whether your equivalent body is a *thing that fights* or a *thing people belong to.* If it is both, say so explicitly and give it two chains — a tactical one and an identity one. That duality is realistic, it is a proven engine of loyalty conflict, and it is exactly the kind of institutional depth Part 28 rewards. What is not defensible is using the word to mean whichever of the two is convenient in a given scene.

### R3.4 — The Marine dimension, briefly

Because the saga has a standing fleet and land forces that interact with it, the marine pattern is worth a line. **US Marine Corps** land organisation follows the army ladder with its own strengths: a rifle squad of thirteen Marines in three four-man fire teams plus a squad leader (a figure the Corps has varied experimentally in recent years and returned to), a platoon of roughly 43, a company of roughly 180 under a Captain, a battalion of roughly 800–1,200 under a Lieutenant Colonel, and — unlike the modern army — **real regiments** of roughly 3,000 under a Colonel inside divisions of roughly 20,000 under a Major General. Above that sits the Marine Expeditionary Force under a Lieutenant General. **The Royal Marines** use British Army rank titles and a **commando** as their battalion-equivalent (roughly 650 under a Lieutenant Colonel), grouped in a brigade.

## R4 — US Army Rank Ladder

Three ladders, not one: enlisted (E-1 to E-9), warrant officer (W-1 to W-5), and commissioned (O-1 to O-10). The pay-grade code is the US Army's own; the NATO code is given for cross-national comparison.

### R4.1 — Enlisted (E-1 to E-9)

| Grade | NATO | Rank | Typical command / role |
|---|---|---|---|
| E-1 | OR-1 | **Private (PVT)** | Entry rank; no insignia. Trainee and junior soldier. |
| E-2 | OR-2 | **Private (PV2)** | Junior soldier. |
| E-3 | OR-3 | **Private First Class (PFC)** | Junior soldier with some experience; a team member, not a leader. |
| E-4 | OR-4 | **Specialist (SPC)** | **Not an NCO.** A technically skilled soldier at the same pay grade as Corporal but outside the NCO chain — the most common rank in the army. |
| E-4 | OR-4 | **Corporal (CPL)** | **The first NCO rank.** Fire team leader. Same pay grade as Specialist, different status entirely. |
| E-5 | OR-5 | **Sergeant (SGT)** | **Fire team leader**, sometimes squad leader. The rank where a soldier becomes responsible for other soldiers' lives and training. |
| E-6 | OR-6 | **Staff Sergeant (SSG)** | **Squad leader** (9 soldiers) or section leader. |
| E-7 | OR-7 | **Sergeant First Class (SFC)** | **Platoon Sergeant** — second-in-command of a platoon and the officer's indispensable partner. |
| E-8 | OR-8 | **Master Sergeant (MSG)** | Senior staff NCO at battalion level and above; a specialist and planner rather than a troop leader. |
| E-8 | OR-8 | **First Sergeant (1SG)** | **The senior enlisted soldier of a company** — the company commander's counterpart, responsible for discipline, welfare, standards and administration of 100–200 soldiers. Same grade as Master Sergeant, different job and different status. |
| E-9 | OR-9 | **Sergeant Major (SGM)** | Senior enlisted staff appointment at high headquarters. |
| E-9 | OR-9 | **Command Sergeant Major (CSM)** | **The senior enlisted soldier of a battalion, brigade, division, corps or army** — the commander's direct adviser on everything to do with soldiers. |
| E-9 (special) | OR-9 | **Sergeant Major of the Army (SMA)** | One post in the entire army: the senior enlisted soldier of the service, adviser to the Chief of Staff. |

**The one point that most often confuses non-Americans:** E-4 exists twice, as **Specialist** and as **Corporal**, at the same pay. A Specialist is a skilled soldier without command responsibility; a Corporal is a leader of a fire team and is inside the NCO chain, with all the authority and accountability that carries. The distinction is status, not money.

### R4.2 — Warrant officers (W-1 to W-5)

| Grade | Rank | Role |
|---|---|---|
| W-1 | **Warrant Officer 1 (WO1)** | Appointed by warrant; a technical specialist entering the warrant track. |
| W-2 | **Chief Warrant Officer 2 (CW2)** | Commissioned from this grade upward. Qualified specialist. |
| W-3 | **Chief Warrant Officer 3 (CW3)** | Advanced specialist; a systems or branch expert. |
| W-4 | **Chief Warrant Officer 4 (CW4)** | Senior specialist, adviser at brigade and above. |
| W-5 | **Chief Warrant Officer 5 (CW5)** | Master-level specialist; the branch's technical authority. |

**What a US warrant officer actually is:** a **deep technical specialist** — an aviator, a maintenance authority, an intelligence or cyber expert, a signals or munitions specialist — occupying a track *between* the enlisted and commissioned ladders. They are saluted, they hold a commission from CW2 upward, they outrank all enlisted soldiers and are junior to all commissioned officers. **They do not normally command troop units.** Their authority comes from expertise rather than position: a CW4 maintenance officer is the person in a brigade who actually knows whether the vehicles will run, and their word on that carries weight no rank can override. The great majority of US Army aviators are warrant officers, and an aviation warrant officer does command an aircraft and its crew.

**This is a completely different institution from a British warrant officer.** See R9.5.

### R4.3 — Commissioned (O-1 to O-10)

| Grade | NATO | Rank | Typical command / appointment |
|---|---|---|---|
| O-1 | OF-1 | **Second Lieutenant (2LT)** | **Platoon leader** — first command, ~30–40 soldiers. |
| O-2 | OF-1 | **First Lieutenant (1LT)** | Platoon leader; company executive officer. |
| O-3 | OF-2 | **Captain (CPT)** | **Company commander** — 100–200 soldiers. Also battalion staff. |
| O-4 | OF-3 | **Major (MAJ)** | Battalion executive officer or S-3 (operations); brigade staff. Rarely a commander. |
| O-5 | OF-4 | **Lieutenant Colonel (LTC)** | **Battalion commander** — 300–1,000 soldiers. |
| O-6 | OF-5 | **Colonel (COL)** | **Brigade commander** — 3,000–5,000 soldiers. Also senior division/corps staff. |
| O-7 | OF-6 | **Brigadier General (BG)** | **The first general officer rank (one star).** Deputy division commander; senior staff. |
| O-8 | OF-7 | **Major General (MG)** | **Division commander** (two stars) — 10,000–20,000. |
| O-9 | OF-8 | **Lieutenant General (LTG)** | **Corps commander** (three stars) — 40,000–90,000. |
| O-10 | OF-9 | **General (GEN)** | **Four stars.** Army command, combatant command, Chief of Staff of the Army. |
| (special) | OF-10 | **General of the Army** | Five stars. Wartime only; dormant since the Second World War generation. |

**The career shape** worth knowing for fiction: an officer commands at O-3 (company), returns to staff at O-4, commands again at O-5 (battalion), returns to staff, commands again at O-6 (brigade). Command is episodic and competitive; most officers never command a battalion, and very few command a brigade.

## R5 — British Army Rank Ladder

Two ladders rather than three: **Other Ranks** (OR-1 to OR-9, which *includes* the warrant officers) and **Commissioned Officers** (OF-1 to OF-10). The absence of a separate warrant-officer ladder is a structural difference from the US Army, not a cosmetic one.

### R5.1 — Other ranks, including warrant officers (OR-1 to OR-9)

| NATO | Rank | Typical command / role |
|---|---|---|
| OR-1 / OR-2 | **Private** | Entry rank. **The title varies by regiment and corps** and is a live piece of identity: Guardsman (Foot Guards), Rifleman (The Rifles), Fusilier, Trooper (cavalry/armour), Gunner (Royal Artillery), Sapper (Royal Engineers), Signaller (Royal Signals), Craftsman (REME), Airtrooper (Army Air Corps), Kingsman, Ranger, Highlander, Private (most others). |
| OR-3 | **Lance Corporal (LCpl)** | **Fire team commander**; second-in-command of a section. The first step into leadership, and in British service a hard-won and jealously held one. |
| OR-4 | **Corporal (Cpl)** | **Section commander** — eight soldiers. In armour, a vehicle commander. |
| OR-6 | **Sergeant (Sgt)** | **Platoon Sergeant** — second-in-command of a platoon, the officer's partner. (Note: **the British Army has no OR-5**; the ladder steps from Corporal at OR-4 to Sergeant at OR-6.) |
| OR-7 | **Staff Sergeant (SSgt)** / **Colour Sergeant (CSgt)** | Company Quartermaster Sergeant; senior specialist; platoon commander in some corps. "Colour Sergeant" is the infantry title, "Staff Sergeant" the title in most other arms. |
| OR-8 | **Warrant Officer Class 2 (WO2)** | Normally holds the appointment of **Company Sergeant Major (CSM)** or **Squadron Sergeant Major (SSM)** — the senior soldier of a company or squadron. |
| OR-9 | **Warrant Officer Class 1 (WO1)** | Normally holds the appointment of **Regimental Sergeant Major (RSM)** — the senior soldier of a battalion or regiment, and one of the most formidable figures in the British military. Other WO1 appointments include Garrison Sergeant Major, Academy Sergeant Major, and the single post of **Army Sergeant Major**, the senior soldier of the whole British Army. |

**British warrant officers are soldiers, not a separate officer class.** They rise through the enlisted ranks over a long career, they hold a warrant from the Sovereign rather than a commission, they are addressed as "Sir" or "Ma'am" by all junior ranks, and officers address the RSM as "Mr [Surname]" or by appointment. A WO1 who is an RSM exercises authority over a battalion's discipline and standards that in practice extends to reprimanding junior officers — a structural fact that produces some of the best-known scenes in British military writing.

### R5.2 — Commissioned officers (OF-1 to OF-10)

| NATO | Rank | Typical command / appointment |
|---|---|---|
| (OF-D) | **Officer Cadet** | Under training at Sandhurst; not yet commissioned. |
| OF-1 | **Second Lieutenant (2Lt)** | **Platoon or troop commander** — first command, ~30. |
| OF-1 | **Lieutenant (Lt)** | Platoon or troop commander with experience; specialist platoon roles. |
| OF-2 | **Captain (Capt)** | **Company or squadron second-in-command**; battalion staff (adjutant, operations). **Not normally a company commander** — this is the divergence from US practice. |
| OF-3 | **Major (Maj)** | **Company, squadron or battery commander** — ~100–120. Also battalion operations officer. |
| OF-4 | **Lieutenant Colonel (Lt Col)** | **Battalion or regiment commander** — ~550–750. The most sought-after command in the army. |
| OF-5 | **Colonel (Col)** | **Primarily a staff rank** — brigade chief of staff, division and higher headquarters, defence appointments. Not a field command rank (see R5.3). |
| OF-6 | **Brigadier (Brig)** | **Brigade commander** — 3,000–5,000. **Not a general officer** in British usage (see R9.4). |
| OF-7 | **Major General (Maj Gen)** | **Division commander** — 10,000–20,000. The first general officer rank in British usage. |
| OF-8 | **Lieutenant General (Lt Gen)** | **Corps commander**; senior joint and defence appointments. |
| OF-9 | **General (Gen)** | Chief of the General Staff; senior joint command. |
| OF-10 | **Field Marshal** | Ceremonial and honorary in modern practice; not held as an active operational rank. |

### R5.3 — Two British peculiarities worth stating explicitly

**Colonel is a staff rank, not a command rank.** A British officer commands a battalion as a Lieutenant Colonel, then goes to a staff appointment as a Colonel, then commands a brigade as a Brigadier. The rank of Colonel sits in the ladder but is generally passed *through* rather than commanded *from*. This is the mirror image of US practice, where Colonel is the brigade command rank and one of the most significant commands in an officer's career.

**The ceremonial Colonel of the Regiment is a different thing again** (see R2.4): a figurehead appointment attached to a regiment, held by a senior or retired officer, carrying no command.

## R6 — Naval Organisation and Rank Ladders

Navies organise around **ships** rather than around blocks of people, which changes the shape of the ladder. The smallest tactical entity is a vessel; formations are groupings of vessels; and the rank ladder is calibrated to the size of ship a person is fit to command.

### R6.1 — Naval organisation, smallest to largest

| Rung | What it is | Typical size | Commander |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Watch section** | The portion of a crew on duty at one time | Varies; a fraction of the crew | The Officer of the Watch / Officer of the Deck |
| **Division** (US) / **Department section** | The basic organisational unit *within* a ship — deck, engineering, weapons, operations | ~10–30 | Division Officer (Ensign to Lieutenant), with a senior Petty Officer or Chief as the working leader |
| **Department** | Several divisions sharing a function (Operations, Engineering, Weapons, Supply, Navigation) | ~30–150 | Department Head (Lieutenant to Lieutenant Commander) |
| **Ship's company** | The whole crew | Patrol boat ~20 · Frigate/destroyer ~200–330 · Cruiser/amphib ~500–1,100 · Aircraft carrier ~3,000–5,000 (plus air wing) | **The Commanding Officer** — always called "the Captain" whatever the rank. A patrol vessel: a Lieutenant. A frigate or destroyer: a **Commander**. A cruiser, amphibious ship or carrier: a **Captain**. |
| **Squadron / Division of ships** | A permanent grouping of like vessels (e.g. a destroyer squadron) | 4–8 ships | **US:** a Captain (O-6) holding the appointment title "Commodore." **RN:** a **Commodore** (OF-6) by rank, or a Captain. |
| **Flotilla** (RN usage) | A larger administrative grouping of ships and squadrons | Variable, many ships | Commodore or Rear Admiral |
| **Task group / Carrier Strike Group** | A task-organised operational force built around a capital ship | ~5–12 ships, 6,000–7,500 personnel | **Rear Admiral** |
| **Task force / Numbered fleet** | A theatre force of several task groups | Dozens of ships | **Vice Admiral** |
| **Fleet / Fleet command** | The service's operational whole in a theatre or overall | Very large | **Admiral** |

**The organising principle** to carry across into fiction: at sea, the meaningful unit is the hull, and the meaningful question about a commander is *what size of hull are they fit to command.* Everything above ship level is task-organised and temporary far more often than in a land force — a task group is assembled for a deployment and dissolved afterwards, which is why naval formations tend to be numbered rather than named and titled.

### R6.2 — US Navy ranks

**Enlisted (E-1 to E-9).** The rating structure combines rank with trade: a sailor is not simply a Petty Officer but a Petty Officer of a specific rating (Gunner's Mate, Machinist's Mate, Hospital Corpsman, and so on).

| Grade | Rank | Note |
|---|---|---|
| E-1 | **Seaman Recruit** | Entry. |
| E-2 | **Seaman Apprentice** | |
| E-3 | **Seaman** | (Or Fireman / Airman / Constructionman, depending on the career field.) |
| E-4 | **Petty Officer Third Class** | **The first NCO-equivalent rank.** |
| E-5 | **Petty Officer Second Class** | |
| E-6 | **Petty Officer First Class** | Leading Petty Officer of a division. |
| E-7 | **Chief Petty Officer** | **A major status threshold** — the Chief's Mess is a distinct institution within a ship, and "making Chief" is the defining transition of an enlisted naval career. |
| E-8 | **Senior Chief Petty Officer** | |
| E-9 | **Master Chief Petty Officer** | Plus the appointments Command Master Chief (a ship or command's senior enlisted sailor) and Master Chief Petty Officer of the Navy (one post in the service). |

**Warrant officers.** Chief Warrant Officer 2 through Chief Warrant Officer 5, drawn from senior enlisted ranks as technical specialists; W-1 is used only in limited specialities. Same principle as the army's (R4.2): expertise rather than command.

**Commissioned (O-1 to O-10).**

| Grade | NATO | Rank | Army equivalent | Typical role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| O-1 | OF-1 | **Ensign** | 2nd Lieutenant | Division officer aboard ship. |
| O-2 | OF-1 | **Lieutenant Junior Grade** | 1st Lieutenant | Division officer. |
| O-3 | OF-2 | **Lieutenant** | Captain | Department head on a small ship; command of a patrol vessel. |
| O-4 | OF-3 | **Lieutenant Commander** | Major | Department head; executive officer. |
| O-5 | OF-4 | **Commander** | Lieutenant Colonel | **Commanding officer of a frigate, destroyer or submarine.** |
| O-6 | OF-5 | **Captain** | Colonel | **Commanding officer of a cruiser, amphibious ship or aircraft carrier**; squadron commander with the title Commodore. |
| O-7 | OF-6 | **Rear Admiral (lower half)** | Brigadier General | One star. Strike group command; senior staff. |
| O-8 | OF-7 | **Rear Admiral (upper half)** | Major General | Two stars. |
| O-9 | OF-8 | **Vice Admiral** | Lieutenant General | Three stars. Numbered fleet command. |
| O-10 | OF-9 | **Admiral** | General | Four stars. Fleet command; Chief of Naval Operations. |
| (special) | OF-10 | **Fleet Admiral** | General of the Army | Five stars. Wartime only; dormant. |

**Commodore** in US service is an *appointment*, not a rank (see R2.4) — with the historical exception of a brief period in the 1980s when it existed as a one-star rank before being renamed Rear Admiral (lower half).

### R6.3 — Royal Navy ranks

**Ratings.**

| NATO | Rank | Note |
|---|---|---|
| OR-2 | **Able Rating (AB)** | Entry and junior rating. (Historically Ordinary Seaman then Able Seaman; the RN has revised these titles more than once.) |
| OR-4 | **Leading Hand (LH)** | The first supervisory rating — sometimes called "Killick" for the anchor on the badge. |
| OR-6 | **Petty Officer (PO)** | Senior rating; equivalent in standing to an army Sergeant. |
| OR-7 | **Chief Petty Officer (CPO)** | Senior rating with substantial departmental responsibility. |
| OR-9 | **Warrant Officer Class 1 (WO1)** | The senior rating of a ship or establishment. *(A Warrant Officer Class 2 grade was introduced in the 2000s and subsequently withdrawn; treat WO1 as the RN's single warrant grade for reference purposes.)* |

**Commissioned officers.**

| NATO | Rank | Army equivalent | Typical role |
|---|---|---|---|
| (OF-D) | **Midshipman** | Officer Cadet | Under training. |
| OF-1 | **Sub-Lieutenant** | 2nd Lieutenant / Lieutenant | Junior officer aboard ship. |
| OF-2 | **Lieutenant (Lt)** | **Captain** | Departmental officer; command of a small vessel — where he is "the Captain" by appointment. |
| OF-3 | **Lieutenant Commander (Lt Cdr)** | **Major** | Head of department; executive officer; command of a minor war vessel. |
| OF-4 | **Commander (Cdr)** | **Lieutenant Colonel** | **Commanding officer of a frigate, destroyer or submarine.** |
| OF-5 | **Captain (Capt)** | **Colonel** | **Commanding officer of a major warship**; senior staff appointments. |
| OF-6 | **Commodore (Cdre)** | **Brigadier** | A **substantive rank** in the RN. Squadron or flotilla command; senior staff. |
| OF-7 | **Rear Admiral (RAdm)** | Major General | Task group command; senior appointments. |
| OF-8 | **Vice Admiral (VAdm)** | Lieutenant General | Fleet Commander; senior joint appointments. |

**The offset that catches everyone.** A naval Lieutenant is **not** an army Lieutenant. The RN and USN Lieutenant sits at OF-2, level with an army **Captain**; a naval Lieutenant Commander is level with a **Major**; a naval Commander with a **Lieutenant Colonel**; a naval Captain with a **Colonel**. The naval ladder is compressed relative to the land ladder because it is calibrated to hulls rather than to headcount. A scene in which an army Lieutenant condescends to a naval Lieutenant has the seniority exactly backwards.

## R7 — Air Organisation and Rank Ladders

Air forces organise around **aircraft and the machinery needed to keep them flying**, which produces a ladder that is shallower than the land ladder and heavily weighted toward the squadron.

### R7.1 — US Air Force organisation, smallest to largest

| Rung | Composition | Typical size | Commander |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Element** | The smallest tactical grouping | 2–4 aircraft, or a handful of personnel | The element lead |
| **Flight** | Several elements, or a functional section on the ground | ~20–100 personnel, or 4–6 aircraft | Captain or Major |
| **Squadron** | 2 or more flights. **The basic unit of the USAF** — the level at which a unit has an identity, a heritage and a mission. | ~50–750 personnel; a flying squadron typically 12–24 aircraft | **Lieutenant Colonel** |
| **Group** | 2 or more squadrons of one function (operations, maintenance, mission support, medical) | Several hundred to a few thousand | **Colonel** |
| **Wing** | Several groups; normally holds one base and one primary mission | ~1,000–5,000+ | **Colonel** or **Brigadier General** |
| **Numbered Air Force (NAF)** | Several wings; a theatre-level operational headquarters | Tens of thousands | **Major General** or **Lieutenant General** |
| **Major Command (MAJCOM)** | The service's top functional or regional commands | Very large | **General** |

**US Space Force**, relevant because the saga has Space Forces, uses a parallel but distinct structure: **squadron**, then **delta** (roughly equivalent to a wing, and organised by mission rather than by base), then **field command** (roughly equivalent to a MAJCOM). Its personnel are Guardians, and its rank titles mirror the Air Force's with the enlisted grades E-1 through E-4 titled **Specialist 1 through Specialist 4**, followed by Sergeant, Technical Sergeant, Master Sergeant, Senior Master Sergeant and Chief Master Sergeant.

### R7.2 — US Air Force ranks

**Enlisted (E-1 to E-9).**

| Grade | Rank | Note |
|---|---|---|

| E-2 | **Airman** | |
| E-3 | **Airman First Class** | |

| E-5 | **Staff Sergeant** | **The first NCO rank in the USAF** — note this is a grade higher than the army's NCO threshold. |
| E-6 | **Technical Sergeant** | |
| E-7 | **Master Sergeant** | Senior NCO; flight-level leadership. |
| E-8 | **Senior Master Sergeant** | |
| E-9 | **Chief Master Sergeant** | Plus Command Chief Master Sergeant (a wing's senior enlisted airman) and Chief Master Sergeant of the Air Force (one post). |

**Warrant officers.** The USAF operated without warrant officers for decades and reintroduced them in 2024 for information-technology and cyber specialities. For most purposes the USAF ladder is two-part rather than three-part.

**Commissioned.** Identical titles to the US Army: Second Lieutenant, First Lieutenant, Captain, Major, Lieutenant Colonel, Colonel, Brigadier General, Major General, Lieutenant General, General.

### R7.3 — Royal Air Force organisation, smallest to largest

| Rung | Composition | Typical size | Commander |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flight** | The smallest formal unit | A few aircraft, or a ground section | **Flight Lieutenant** or **Squadron Leader** |

## R8 — Cross-Service and Cross-National Equivalence

The NATO code is the interoperability key: **the same code means the same level of seniority across every service and both nations**, whatever the local title. This single table is the fastest way to check whether a fictional rank sits at the level a scene needs.

### R8.1 — Commissioned officers (OF-1 to OF-10)

| NATO | US Army / USAF | British Army | Royal Navy | US Navy | Royal Air Force | Commands (land) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **OF-1** | 2nd Lieutenant · 1st Lieutenant | 2nd Lieutenant · Lieutenant | Midshipman · Sub-Lieutenant | Ensign · Lieutenant JG | Pilot Officer · Flying Officer | Platoon / troop |
| **OF-2** | Captain | Captain | **Lieutenant** | **Lieutenant** | Flight Lieutenant | Company (US) / company 2IC (UK) |
| **OF-3** | Major | Major | **Lieutenant Commander** | **Lieutenant Commander** | Squadron Leader | Company (UK) / battalion staff (US) |
| **OF-4** | Lieutenant Colonel | Lieutenant Colonel | **Commander** | **Commander** | Wing Commander | Battalion / regiment (UK) |
| **OF-5** | Colonel | Colonel | **Captain** | **Captain** | Group Captain | Brigade (US) / staff (UK) |
| **OF-6** | Brigadier General | **Brigadier** | **Commodore** | Rear Admiral (lower half) | Air Commodore | Brigade (UK) |
| **OF-7** | Major General | Major General | Rear Admiral | Rear Admiral (upper half) | Air Vice-Marshal | Division |
| **OF-8** | Lieutenant General | Lieutenant General | Vice Admiral | Vice Admiral | Air Marshal | Corps |
| **OF-9** | General | General | Admiral | Admiral | Air Chief Marshal | Army / service chief |
| **OF-10** | General of the Army | Field Marshal | Admiral of the Fleet | Fleet Admiral | Marshal of the RAF | Army group / ceremonial |

*(The US pay-grade numbering runs one ahead of the NATO code above OF-1 because the US splits OF-1 into two grades, O-1 and O-2. So a US O-6 Colonel and a British Colonel are both OF-5; a US O-7 Brigadier General and a British Brigadier are both OF-6.)*

### R8.2 — Enlisted and other ranks (OR-1 to OR-9)

| NATO | US Army | British Army | US Navy | Royal Navy | Typical command |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **OR-1/2** | Private (E-1/E-2) | Private (and regimental variants) | Seaman Recruit / Apprentice | Able Rating | — |
| **OR-3** | Private First Class (E-3) | **Lance Corporal** | Seaman | — | Fire team (UK) |

| **OR-8** | Master Sergeant · **First Sergeant** (E-8) | **Warrant Officer Class 2** (CSM) | Senior Chief Petty Officer | — | Company senior enlisted |
| **OR-9** | Sergeant Major · **Command Sergeant Major** (E-9) | **Warrant Officer Class 1** (RSM) | Master Chief Petty Officer | Warrant Officer Class 1 | Battalion and above senior enlisted |

**Note the shape of the divergence.** The British ladder puts its warrant officers at OR-8 and OR-9, *inside* the other-ranks structure, as the culmination of a soldier's career. The US ladder puts Master Sergeant / First Sergeant and Sergeant Major / Command Sergeant Major at those codes, and keeps its warrant officers on a **separate ladder entirely** between the enlisted and commissioned structures. The two nations use the same words for entirely different institutions (see R9.5).

## R9 — The Common Errors, and the Genuine Divergences

These are the traps. Each one is a real difference between the two national systems — not a matter of style — and each is a place where fiction routinely goes wrong.

### R9.1 — A UK regiment is not a US regiment

Covered in full at R3.3. Short form: in the British Army a **regiment** is either a permanent administrative and identity body containing battalions (infantry), or a battalion-sized tactical unit subdivided into squadrons or batteries (armour, artillery, engineers, signals, logistics). In the modern US Army it is chiefly a **lineage device** with a handful of genuine exceptions. There is no reading of "regiment" that means the same thing in both armies.

### R9.2 — A UK company is major-commanded; a US company is captain-commanded

The British Army places one rank more seniority at company level than the US Army does. **A British Captain is a company second-in-command or a staff officer; a US Captain commands the company.** This propagates upward, and is the underlying cause of R9.3 and R9.4.

### R9.3 — A UK Colonel is a staff rank

A British officer commands a battalion at Lieutenant Colonel and a brigade at Brigadier, passing *through* Colonel as a staff appointment in between. **In the US Army, Colonel commands the brigade.** Writing a British-flavoured Colonel who commands a formation in the field is a recognisable error; so is writing a US Colonel who has no command.

### R9.4 — A UK Brigadier is not a general officer

**Brigadier** (UK, OF-6) is the most senior *field officer* rank, above Colonel and below Major General; British usage does not count it among the general officers. **Brigadier General** (US, O-7/OF-6) sits at the same NATO code but **is** the first general officer rank in US usage, and wears one star. Same seniority, different category, different form of address, and a different meaning attached to reaching it.

### R9.5 — A US warrant officer and a British warrant officer are entirely different institutions

This one produces the most confusion of any item in this file.

| | **US warrant officer** | **British warrant officer** |
|---|---|---|
| Where in the hierarchy | A **separate ladder** between enlisted and commissioned | The **top two rungs of the enlisted ladder** (OR-8, OR-9) |
| What they are | A deep technical specialist — aviator, maintenance, intelligence, cyber, signals | The most senior and experienced soldiers in the unit |
| What they do | Provide expertise; command aircraft; rarely command troop units | Hold the great sergeant-major appointments — CSM, RSM — running a unit's discipline, standards and welfare |
| Authority derives from | Technical mastery | Position, experience and the force of the appointment |
| Career path | Often recruited directly into the warrant track, or from senior enlisted | Always risen through the enlisted ranks over 15–25 years |
| Address | "Mister/Chief" | "Sir"/"Ma'am" from juniors; "Mr [Surname]" or by appointment from officers |

A fictional force may adopt either model — or a third — but it should adopt **one** and be consistent, because the two produce completely different characters occupying the same rank name.

### R9.6 — A naval Lieutenant outranks an army Lieutenant by one NATO grade

Covered at R6.3. A naval Lieutenant is OF-2, level with an army **Captain**, one NATO grade above an army Lieutenant (OF-1) and two above a Second Lieutenant. The naval ladder is compressed relative to the land ladder throughout: Lieutenant Commander is level with Major, Commander with Lieutenant Colonel, Captain with Colonel.

### R9.7 — "Captain" is two different things in the same room

A naval **Captain** by rank is OF-5, level with an army Colonel. The **captain** of a ship is whoever commands it, at any rank from Lieutenant upward, addressed as Captain by appointment. Both can be present in a scene, and the ship's captain has absolute authority aboard his own vessel regardless of the visiting Captain's seniority.

### R9.8 — "Squadron," "troop," and "section" name different rungs in different services

Covered at R2.5. A US cavalry **troop** is company-sized; a UK armoured **troop** is platoon-sized. A UK armoured **squadron** is company-sized; an RAF **squadron** is commanded by an OF-4 and a naval **squadron** is a group of ships. A British **section** is eight soldiers; a US Navy **section** is a watch subdivision. Where a fictional force reuses one word across branches, it must decide which rung the word names in each and hold to it.

### R9.9 — The USAF NCO threshold is a grade higher than the Army's

A US Army soldier becomes an NCO at **Corporal (E-4)**; a US airman becomes an NCO at **Staff Sergeant (E-5)**, with Senior Airman (E-4) still a junior rank. The same grade number means different status in the two services.

### R9.10 — Paper strength is not fighting strength

Covered at R1.2 and R1.3. This is the error that most distorts scale in fiction: treating establishment figures as the number of people who will actually be at the objective. Between manning shortfall, detachments, casualties, and the support tail, the number of rifles at the point of contact is a fraction of the formation's headcount — and the gap between the two is one of the most reliable sources of institutional tension a military story has.
